\begin{abstract}
While surveillance camera networks offer high-coverage visual feeds, no existing framework jointly addresses camera-level vehicle perception and network-wide traffic forecasting under motorcycle-dominant mixed traffic. We establish a comprehensive camera-network benchmark for motorcycle-dominant traffic, addressing a critical gap in developing nations. Evaluated on a 608-node camera network in Ho Chi Minh City, we propose a two-stage, privacy-preserving framework. Stage 1 utilizes an edge-deployed EfficientNet-B4 for direct vehicle count regression, ensuring no personally identifiable information is transmitted. Stage 2 introduces \emph{TA-STGCN} (Temporal Attention-Guided Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network), coupling graph convolutions with temporal self-attention to adaptively filter two-stage perception noise. Multi-seed evaluations demonstrate that while TA-STGCN achieves on-par point-forecasting accuracy ($\text{MAE} = 3.2401 \pm 0.0108$), its primary architectural contribution lies in mitigation of two-stage error propagation and training stability ($\text{CV} = 0.23\%$). By dynamically suppressing high-frequency perception noise, TA-STGCN significantly outperforms baselines under extreme camera noise conditions (improving by up to $4.95$ MAE over GraphWaveNet). Furthermore, it maintains a compact parameter footprint of 453K parameters while requiring $25.2\%$ less peak memory than Transformer baselines like STAEformer, making it well-suited for resource-constrained Edge-Cloud deployments.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Rapid global urbanization and the surging number of personal vehicles—especially passenger cars and motorcycles—cause severe traffic congestion in fast-growing metropolitan areas like Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. Since expanding physical road infrastructure is prohibitively slow and expensive, Artificial Intelligence (AI) augmented Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS) emerge as a cost-effective solution for real-time traffic perception and proactive congestion forecasting.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig/traffic_jam.jpg}
\caption{Urban traffic congestion in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. This scene illustrates the motorcycle-dominant mixed traffic challenge, characterized by highly unstructured lateral movements, lack of strict lane discipline, and severe visual occlusions between passenger cars and motorcycles.}
\label{fig:traffic_jam}
\end{figure}

In modern urban environments, surveillance camera networks offer high-coverage visual feeds at lower cost than physical induction loop sensors. Transforming raw visual camera feeds into proactive city-wide traffic intelligence requires two sequential stages: (1) \textbf{Camera-Level Perception} (estimating category-specific vehicle counts from individual feeds) and (2) \textbf{Network-Wide Forecasting} (forecasting traffic flow trends across the interconnected road graph). Independent single-node forecasting is inadequate because congestion propagates to connected downstream road segments \cite{ctm}.

To address Stage 2, rather than proposing an entirely novel architecture for marginal point-accuracy gains, we adapt the highly efficient STGCN \cite{stgcn} into \textbf{TA-STGCN} (Temporal Attention-Guided Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network). By coupling Chebyshev Spectral Graph Convolutions \cite{chebnet} with a Temporal Self-Attention module \cite{attention}, TA-STGCN functions as an adaptive dynamic filter specifically designed to suppress the high-frequency temporal noise inherent to two-stage visual vehicle perception. We provide theoretical intuition and empirical motivation (Section~\ref{sec:methods}) showing that this mechanism provides an adaptive variance-bias trade-off to stabilize long-range dependencies and prevent error cascades.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Camera-Sensed Framework for Mixed Traffic:} We design a two-stage ITS framework that bridges camera-level visual perception (Stage 1) with network-wide spatio-temporal graph forecasting (Stage 2), directly addressing the unique operational challenges of motorcycle-dominant mixed traffic in developing nations without relying on expensive inductive loops.
    \item \textbf{Annotated Camera Vehicle Image Dataset:} We contribute a real-world dataset of 5,012 manually annotated traffic camera images (4,010 Train, 501 Validation, 501 Test) collected across major intersections in Ho Chi Minh City, providing continuous count annotations for passenger cars and motorcycles.
    \item \textbf{City-Scale Camera-Only Traffic Graph:} To address the pronounced scarcity of empirical datasets capturing heterogeneous mixed-traffic dynamics in developing nations, this work establishes a large-scale 608-node city-scale traffic forecasting benchmark. Derived entirely from municipal camera infrastructure, the graph is constructed via automated multi-threaded API pipelines harvesting data from 657 CCTV cameras, integrated with topological road distances and RBF-weighted adjacency matrices.
    \item \textbf{Noise Resilience and Two-Stage Error Cascade Mitigation:} We design \emph{TA-STGCN}, introducing Temporal Self-Attention \cite{attention} between ST-Conv blocks. Rather than claiming a fundamentally novel theoretical breakthrough for raw accuracy, TA-STGCN is designed as a pragmatic architectural adaptation specifically tailored to solve two-stage error propagation. It acts as an adaptive dynamic filter, mitigating perception noise propagated from Stage 1, yielding strong robustness under extreme noise conditions and stabilizing forecasts at long horizons.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Seed Benchmark for Edge-Cloud Deployments:} We conduct baseline comparisons across 5 independent random seeds. Beyond standard point-accuracy metrics, we evaluate models based on noise resilience, variance stability, and hardware efficiency. TA-STGCN establishes itself as a highly reliable architecture for real-world Edge-Cloud deployments, avoiding the severe degradation seen in heavy baselines when camera inputs become noisy or corrupted ($\text{CV} = 0.23\%$).
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the datasets, the two-stage methodology, and the experimental configuration. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the experimental results. Section~\ref{sec:deployment_limitations} discusses the implications, hardware efficiency, and deployment limitations. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Building city-scale traffic intelligence from camera networks requires progress on two coupled fronts: extracting reliable vehicle measurements from visual feeds, and forecasting their spatio-temporal evolution. However, most existing literature addresses these problems under conditions that differ sharply from the camera-sensed, motorcycle-dominant urban traffic this study targets. We summarize this gap in Table~\ref{tab:related_comparison}.

\subsection{Visual Vehicle Counting Under Dense Traffic}
Visual vehicle counting traditionally relies on Tracking-by-Detection pipelines (e.g., YOLO + ByteTrack \cite{yolo}) or Density Map Estimation (e.g., MCNN \cite{mcnn}, CSRNet \cite{csrnet}, CAN \cite{can}). However, these prevailing paradigms are fundamentally ill-suited for the environment targeted in this study for three reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Extreme Mutual Occlusion:} In developing nations, motorcycles do not adhere to strict lane discipline, resulting in 80--90\% physical occlusion during congestion. Bounding boxes overlap catastrophically, triggering severe Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) bottlenecks that blind standard object detectors.
    \item \textbf{Temporal Discontinuity:} Public municipal camera APIs typically provide static image snapshots at low polling frequencies (e.g., 1 frame per minute) to conserve bandwidth. This severe temporal sparsity breaks the fundamental continuous-frame assumption required for Tracking-by-Detection algorithms to function.
    \item \textbf{Edge Hardware Constraints:} Running heavy multi-object tracking pipelines per camera node is economically prohibitive for city-scale deployments. Density maps are computationally lighter but inherently class-agnostic, destroying the vital car-vs-motorcycle distinction.
\end{enumerate}

To bypass these limitations, we adopt a \textbf{Direct Regression} approach. By utilizing lightweight architectures (e.g., EfficientNet-B4 \cite{efficientnet}) deployed directly on cheap edge hardware, we map static camera images directly to continuous, category-preserving vehicle counts: $I \to [\hat{c}_{\text{car}}, \hat{c}_{\text{bike}}]^T$. While Direct Regression is occasionally criticized for being susceptible to overfitting on global background contexts (e.g., lighting or road geometry) rather than actively counting objects, our qualitative feature attribution analysis (via Grad-CAM++ in Section~\ref{sec:results}) explicitly confirms that the backbone accurately anchors its activations strictly on active vehicle clusters.

\subsection{Spatio-Temporal Traffic Flow Forecasting}
Traffic forecasting has evolved from traditional statistical baselines (e.g., ARIMA \cite{arima}, SVR \cite{svr}) to sequential RNNs (e.g., DCRNN \cite{dcrnn}, T-GCN \cite{tgcn}, GC-LSTM \cite{gclstm}), spectral GCNs (e.g., Kipf \& Welling \cite{gcn}), and modern STGNN architectures (e.g., STGCN \cite{stgcn}, GraphWaveNet \cite{wavenet}, STID \cite{stid}, TPA-LSTM \cite{tpalstm}, ST-GNCDE \cite{stgncde}). Detailed taxonomies of spatio-temporal deep learning paradigms are provided in comprehensive surveys \cite{yin_survey, gnn_survey}. While convolutional models are highly efficient, their localized 1D temporal receptive fields struggle with non-stationary long-range dependencies. Conversely, pure self-attention Transformers (e.g., ASTGCN \cite{astgcn}, STAEformer \cite{staeformer}) achieve high accuracy but incur prohibitive quadratic spatial-temporal memory footprints across large urban graphs.

\subsection{Hybrid Architectures and Adaptive Graphs}
To bridge the gap between convolutional efficiency and transformer accuracy, recent hybrid architectures explore mixing CNNs and Transformers (e.g., PDFormer \cite{pdformer}). Additionally, recognizing the limitations of static distance-based graphs, modern STGNNs increasingly utilize dynamic or adaptive adjacency matrices to capture shifting correlations (e.g., STAG-GCN \cite{staggcn}, STJGCN \cite{stjgcn}). However, these methods often remain computationally expensive. TA-STGCN contributes to this hybrid paradigm by explicitly decoupling spatial Chebyshev graph convolutions from temporal self-attention, creating an optimal variance-bias trade-off for edge-to-cloud deployments. Furthermore, modern Intelligent Transportation Systems face stringent public privacy requirements. Traditional tracking-by-detection systems require transmitting raw video or license plates to central servers, posing severe privacy risks. By employing direct regression at the edge (Stage 1), our framework inherently preserves privacy: only aggregated, anonymized numerical payloads (vehicle counts) are transmitted to the cloud forecasting network (Stage 2).

\subsection{Commercial Systems and Camera-Based Infrastructure}
While commercial platforms like Uber Movement and Google Maps provide real-time congestion data, their proprietary routing algorithms and lack of granular volume metrics make them unsuitable for open municipal forecasting benchmarks. Recently, camera-to-forecast pipelines have gained traction (e.g., \cite{feng2024}, \cite{liu2024}), yet they primarily address structured, car-dominant environments. Similarly, while localized camera-based traffic studies exist in Southeast Asian metropolises like Bangkok and Hanoi \cite{vu2026, nguyen2025}, they primarily focus on isolated intersections rather than continuous, city-scale graph forecasting.

\subsection{The Key Challenge: Mixed, Motorcycle-Dominant Traffic}
\label{sec:gap}

The fundamental limitation of prevailing architectures is their development on highly structured, car-dominant highway benchmarks (e.g., METR-LA, PEMS-BAY) collected via induction loop detectors \cite{dcrnn, stgcn}. These datasets assume predictable fluid-dynamic flows and strict lane discipline. 

In contrast, urban traffic in Southeast Asian metropolises is heterogeneous and motorcycle-dominant. As established in classical and modern traffic studies \cite{minh2003, cao2007, lee2015, mixedtraffic_stgnn}, vehicles cluster densely without lane adherence, causing highly non-stationary temporal fluctuations and sudden spatial spillovers. Because camera-sensed counts exhibit inherent transient perception noise during severe occlusions, applying rigid convolutional architectures directly to this data propagates estimation errors across multi-step forecasts.

As Table~\ref{tab:related_comparison} makes explicit, no existing benchmark addresses the combined challenge of camera-only sensing and heterogeneous mixed traffic at a network-wide scale. This study addresses this gap by coupling category-preserving perception with a parameter-efficient temporal-attention forecasting architecture evaluated on a real 608-node city-scale camera network.

\subsection{Stage 1: Vehicle Counting}
\label{sec:subproblem1}

Stage 1 maps individual camera images to continuous vehicle counts per category $[\hat{c}_{\text{car}}, \hat{c}_{\text{bike}}]^T$. To identify the optimal visual backbone for motorcycle-dominant traffic scenes, we conduct a comprehensive benchmark across four representative deep learning architectures spanning three paradigm families: convolutional (ResNet-50 \cite{resnet}, EfficientNet-B4 \cite{efficientnet}, ConvNeXt-Tiny \cite{convnext}) and attention-based (ViT-Small \cite{vit}).

\subsubsection{ResNet-50 Baseline Backbone}
ResNet-50 \cite{resnet} is utilized as a standard baseline. We project the input image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 224 \times 224}$ into a 2048-dimensional feature representation via Global Average Pooling (GAP):
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{resnet}} = \text{GAP}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\text{ResNet50}}(I)\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{2048}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{EfficientNet-B4 Backbone}
EfficientNet-B4 \cite{efficientnet} applies compound scaling (depth, width, resolution) optimized via neural architecture search. We extract:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{eff}} = \text{GAP}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\text{EfficientNet\text{-}B4}}(I)\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{1792}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{ViT-Small Backbone}
Vision Transformer Small (ViT-S) \cite{vit} tokenizes the input image into $16 \times 16$ patches and applies multi-head self-attention across the patch sequence. We extract the \texttt{[CLS]} token representation:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{vit}} = \mathcal{F}_{\text{ViT\text{-}S}}(I)_{\texttt{[CLS]}} \in \mathbb{R}^{384}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{ConvNeXt-Tiny Backbone}
ConvNeXt-Tiny \cite{convnext} modernizes pure convolutional designs with depthwise separable $7 \times 7$ convolutions, inverted bottleneck blocks, and LayerNorm:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{cnxt}} = \text{GAP}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\text{ConvNeXt}}(I)\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{768}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Unified MLP Regression Head}
For all four backbones, the ImageNet classification layer is removed and replaced by a unified Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) regression head. Given a backbone feature vector $\mathbf{f} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ (where $d$ varies by architecture):
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_1 = \text{LayerNorm}\left( \mathbf{W}_1 \mathbf{f} + \mathbf{b}_1 \right), \quad \mathbf{W}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{128 \times d},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{c}} = \mathbf{W}_2 \cdot \text{ReLU}(\mathbf{h}_1) + \mathbf{b}_2, \quad \mathbf{W}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 128},
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mathbf{c}} = [\hat{c}_{\text{car}}, \hat{c}_{\text{bike}}]^T \in \mathbb{R}^2$ represents the estimated continuous counts of passenger cars and motorcycles in image $I$. Using a shared regression head isolates the effect of the visual backbone from the task-specific head, ensuring a fair comparison across architectures.

\subsubsection{Objective Function}
Stage 1 is trained end-to-end using the Smooth $L_1$ (Huber) Loss \cite{huber} to provide robustness against annotation noise in dense traffic scenes:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{vision}}(\mathbf{c}, \hat{\mathbf{c}}) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{m \in \{\text{car}, \text{bike}\}} \text{Smooth}_{L_1}\left(c_m - \hat{c}_m\right),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\text{Smooth}_{L_1}(x) = \begin{cases} 0.5 x^2, & \text{if } |x| < 1 \\ |x| - 0.5, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Stage 2: Traffic Graph and TA-STGCN}
\label{sec:stage2}
\label{sec:graph_tastgcn}

To capture spatial traffic spillover across the city, estimated vehicle counts across camera locations are integrated into an urban road network graph. It is worth noting that due to the time-series data preprocessing, the initial $657$ camera feeds were reduced to $608$ active nodes (the detailed preprocessing pipeline will be further discussed in Section~\ref{sec:datasets}). The entire camera network is modeled as a directed weighted spatial graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, W)$, where $\mathcal{V}$ represents the set of camera nodes ($N=608$ active camera stations across Ho Chi Minh City), $\mathcal{E}$ represents directed road connections, and $W$ is a Radial Basis Function (RBF) weighted distance matrix.

\subsubsection{Graph Edges and RBF Adjacency Matrix}
Graph edges $\mathcal{E}$ represent physical directed road connections between adjacent camera stations ($2,452$ valid directed edges). To reflect real-world urban road topology—where physical barriers (such as the Saigon River, expressways, and flyovers) or one-way traffic restrictions prevent unconstrained spatial spillover—directed edges $(i,j) \in \mathcal{E}$ are established strictly along valid physical traffic flow corridors derived from OpenStreetMap (OSM) network routing topology. Specifically, a directed edge is only created if there is a direct route from node $i$ to node $j$ without traversing any other intermediate camera node.

For any valid directed connection $(i,j) \in \mathcal{E}$, edge weight $W_{ij} \in [0, 1]$ is computed using a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel based on $d_{ij}$, which denotes the shortest-path network routing distance along valid physical road segments between node $i$ and node $j$ (in kilometers):
\begin{equation}
W_{ij} = \begin{cases} \exp\left(-\frac{d_{ij}^2}{\sigma^2}\right), & \text{if } (i,j) \in \mathcal{E} \text{ and } d_{ij} \le \tau \\ 0, & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of valid road network distances ($\sigma = 1.0887$ km), and $\tau$ is the spatial distance cutoff threshold ($\tau = 3.3945$ km). If no physical road link connects node $i$ to node $j$, or if one-way traffic constraints prohibit direct movement from $i$ to $j$, $W_{ij} = 0$, ensuring that spatial graph convolutions propagate feature representations strictly along physically permissible urban flow paths. This RBF formulation ensures that edge weights fall predominantly between $0$ and $1$, reflecting the tight spatial proximity and strong traffic correlation among the cameras.

\paragraph{Methodological Justification for Static Physical RBF Adjacency vs. Dynamic Graph Learning}
Recent spatio-temporal graph architectures (e.g., GraphWaveNet \cite{wavenet}, DSTAGNN \cite{dstagnn}) often employ dynamic or adaptive graph learning mechanisms, inferring spatial adjacency matrices directly from input node feature correlations via soft-attention or node embedding projections. While adaptive graph learning captures non-physical spatial correlations on clean data, we intentionally restrict TA-STGCN to a \textbf{Static OpenStreetMap (OSM) RBF Physical Adjacency Matrix} for two critical architectural reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Preventing Spurious Spatial Correlations under Perception Noise:} In two-stage camera-sensed ITS pipelines, Stage 2 graph inputs inherently contain Stage 1 visual estimation errors (occlusion noise, transient camera dropouts). Dynamic graph learning algorithms optimize adjacency matrices based on temporal count correlations; consequently, when two geographically distant camera nodes experience simultaneous transient perception noise or lighting shifts, dynamic graph modules mistakenly infer strong artificial spatial edges between them. These \emph{spurious spatial correlations} severely destabilize spatial graph convolutions and trigger rapid error cascading across the network. By enforcing a rigid, physical road-distance cutoff ($\tau = 3.3945$ km) grounded in OSM routing topology, the static physical graph functions as a \textbf{Hard Spatial Anchor}, strictly constraining message passing along physically permissible flow paths and preventing noise-induced correlation collapse.
    \item \textbf{Memory and Computational Efficiency for Edge-Cloud Deployment:} Dynamic graph learning incurs quadratic memory complexity $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ to store and update dense $N \times N$ dynamic attention maps at every forward pass. For a city-scale network ($N=608$), this significantly inflates peak GPU activation memory (e.g., $11,509.1$ MB for STAEformer and $9,801.5$ MB for DSTAGNN). The static physical graph eliminates dynamic adjacency computation entirely, reducing peak activation memory to $8,606.9$ MB ($25.2\%$ lower than STAEformer) and enabling lightweight Edge-Cloud deployment without compromising structural spatial fidelity.
\end{enumerate}

To prepare the adjacency matrix for the Graph Convolutional Network (GCN), the weight matrix is symmetrically normalized. The normalized Graph Laplacian $L$ and Scaled Laplacian $\tilde{L}$ are computed as:
\begin{equation}
L = I_N - D^{-1/2} W D^{-1/2}, \quad \tilde{L} = \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}} L - I_N,
\end{equation}
where $D_{ii} = \sum_{j} W_{ij}$ is the diagonal degree matrix and $\lambda_{\max}$ is the maximum eigenvalue of $L$.

\subsubsection{Historical Feature Tensor Construction}
To model temporal congestion evolution across the network, the graph forecasting model receives a sliding window of historical node features. Let $\Delta t = 5$ minutes denote the temporal resolution of the graph sequence. While finer resolutions (e.g., 1-minute) can capture microscopic traffic dynamics, they introduce extreme temporal sparsity (zero-vehicle frames), amplify high-frequency sensor jitter caused by edge-device API dropouts, and drastically quadruple the sequence length, overwhelming GPU memory. A 5-minute aggregation provides the optimal macroscopic stabilization necessary to suppress perception noise while maintaining sufficient temporal granularity for predicting impending congestion. The input feature tensor $\mathcal{X}^{(t)}$ at time $t$ encompasses the previous $T_{\text{in}}$ observations:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{X}^{(t)} = \left[ X_{t-T_{\text{in}}+1}, X_{t-T_{\text{in}}+2}, \dots, X_t \right] \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times T_{\text{in}} \times F}
\end{equation}
where $N = 608$ is the number of nodes, $T_{\text{in}} = 24$ specifies a 120-minute historical receptive field, and $F = 5$ represents the input feature dimension (passenger car, motorcycle, and temporal encodings). 

In real-world camera deployments, transient API connection failures or camera downtimes occasionally result in missing visual payloads. To maintain the structural integrity of the temporal sequence, missing data points at any node $v_i$ are imputed using Linear Temporal Interpolation across the adjacent available time steps prior to Stage 2 tensor construction. Each node $v_i$ contains a feature vector $\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(t)} \in \mathbb{R}^F$ at time step $t$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Passenger car count $c_{\text{car}}^{(i,t)}$.
    \item Motorcycle count $c_{\text{bike}}^{(i,t)}$.
    \item Sine time-of-day feature: $\sin\left(\frac{2\pi \cdot \text{tod}}{1440}\right)$.
    \item Cosine time-of-day feature: $\cos\left(\frac{2\pi \cdot \text{tod}}{1440}\right)$.
    \item Normalized hour of day: $\frac{\text{hour}}{24}$.
\end{enumerate}

Given historical time series over $T_{\text{in}} = 24$ steps (120 minutes of history), the historical input tensor is $X \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times T_{\text{in}} \times N \times F}$ ($F=5$). The target is forecasting future category-specific traffic volumes across horizon $H = 6$ steps (30 minutes ahead): $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times H \times N \times 2}$.

TA-STGCN couples Chebyshev Spectral Graph Convolutions \cite{chebnet}, 1D Gated Temporal Convolutions (GLU) \cite{stgcn}, and Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention \cite{attention}. Figure~\ref{fig:ta_stgcn_architecture} illustrates the structural diagram of the proposed TA-STGCN architecture.

(a) The overall forecasting pipeline processes $F=5$ input features through 2 ST-Conv blocks with a Temporal Self-Attention module inserted in the middle ($C=80$, Depth=2). (b) The Temporal Self-Attention block establishes a global receptive field via multi-head attention ($h=4$) and a feed-forward network to filter high-frequency perception noise before final output projection.}

TA-STGCN extends the standard Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (STGCN) \cite{stgcn}. The core architecture extracts spatio-temporal features using 2 ST-Conv blocks ($\text{Depth}=2$) composed of 1D Gated Temporal Convolutions (GLU) and Chebyshev Spectral Graph Convolutions ($K=3$) \cite{chebnet} with $C=80$ hidden channels. While 1D convolutions efficiently capture localized transitions, their finite receptive fields struggle under motorcycle-dominant mixed traffic. Dense motorcycle streams generate high-frequency count fluctuations and temporary camera occlusions, which rigid 1D convolutions propagate as estimation noise into multi-step forecasts.

To address this, TA-STGCN inserts a \textbf{Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention} module \cite{attention} ($h=4$ attention heads) in the middle position (between the first and second ST-Conv blocks) globally across the temporal sequence dimension ($T_{\text{in}} = 24$). By computing pairwise temporal affinity weights $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ via the Softmax operation across all historical steps, the self-attention mechanism establishes a constant-time global receptive field. We ground this design in the following heuristic motivation regarding the variance-bias trade-off:

\textbf{Heuristic Motivation (Adaptive Variance-Bias Trade-off):} 
Let $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{s} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_{\text{in}}}$ be an observed traffic time-series vector consisting of the ground-truth traffic signal $\mathbf{s}$ perturbed by independent perception noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma_{\epsilon}^2 \mathbf{I}_{T_{\text{in}}})$. For any attention weights $\boldsymbol{\alpha} \in \Delta^{T_{\text{in}}-1}$ (where $\sum_t \alpha_t = 1$), a standard property of convex combinations dictates that the variance of the attention-weighted noise satisfies:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:attn_var}
\text{Var}\left( \sum_{t=1}^{T_{\text{in}}} \alpha_t \epsilon_t \right) = \sigma_{\epsilon}^2 \sum_{t=1}^{T_{\text{in}}} \alpha_t^2 = \frac{\sigma_{\epsilon}^2}{T_{\text{eff}}},
\end{equation}
where $T_{\text{eff}} = 1 / \|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_2^2 \in [1, T_{\text{in}}]$ is the effective attention window. 

While simple average pooling ($\alpha_t = 1/T_{\text{in}}$) mathematically minimizes variance by maximizing $T_{\text{eff}}$, it introduces severe temporal bias (blurring critical traffic shifts). We hypothesize that the Multi-Head Temporal Attention mechanism empirically exploits this property to dynamically optimize the trade-off. Furthermore, the use of multiple attention heads ($h=4$) provides subspace diversity, allowing the model to simultaneously capture different $T_{\text{eff}}$ scales across different heads. Note that while this heuristic relies on an idealized Gaussian noise assumption to illustrate the bounding mechanism, empirical perception noise in mixed traffic is strictly heteroscedastic and asymmetric; consequently, we validate this adaptive bounding property via extensive empirical noise simulations in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

Formally, the Temporal Self-Attention module computes scaled dot-product attention over the temporal sequence dimension. Given the reshaped hidden representation $\mathcal{H}_{\text{seq}} \in \mathbb{R}^{(B \times N) \times T_{\text{in}} \times C}$, the single-head attention output is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:attn_single}
\text{Attn}(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}) = \text{Softmax}\left( \frac{\mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^{\mathsf{T}}}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right) \mathbf{V},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{Q} = \mathcal{H}_{\text{seq}}\mathbf{W}_Q$, $\mathbf{K} = \mathcal{H}_{\text{seq}}\mathbf{W}_K$, $\mathbf{V} = \mathcal{H}_{\text{seq}}\mathbf{W}_V$ are the query, key, and value projections, and $d_k = C / h$ is the per-head dimension. To capture diverse temporal affinity patterns simultaneously, we adopt \textbf{multi-head attention with $h=4$ heads}, which concatenates the outputs of $h$ parallel attention heads and projects them back to the model dimension:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:attn_multi}
\text{MultiHead}(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}) = \text{Concat}\left( \text{head}_1, \dots, \text{head}_h \right) \mathbf{W}_O, \quad \text{head}_i = \text{Attn}\left( \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}_Q^{(i)}, \mathbf{K}\mathbf{W}_K^{(i)}, \mathbf{V}\mathbf{W}_V^{(i)} \right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}_Q^{(i)}, \mathbf{W}_K^{(i)}, \mathbf{W}_V^{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times d_k}$ and $\mathbf{W}_O \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times C}$. Each head operates in a distinct $d_k = C/h = 20$-dimensional subspace, enabling the model to attend to different temporal scales and noise patterns in parallel. We select $h=4$ as the canonical configuration because it balances subspace diversity against parameter overhead: our ablation study (Section~\ref{sec:results}) shows that $h=4$ outperforms both the single-head variant ($h=1$) and the over-parameterized $h=8$ variant, confirming that four heads provide sufficient representational capacity without inducing redundant projections.

The attention output tensor is then passed through a 1D temporal convolution to collapse the historical time dimension into the target forecast horizon $H=6$, producing predictions $\hat{Y}$. The network is optimized end-to-end using the Huber Loss \cite{huber}.

Algorithm~\ref{algo:ta_stgcn_forward} details the exact forward pass operations and tensor shape transformations of the TA-STGCN architecture.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{TA-STGCN Forward Pass (Depth=2, Middle Temporal Attention)}
\label{algo:ta_stgcn_forward}
\DontPrintSemicolon
\KwIn{Historical traffic tensor $\mathcal{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times T_{\text{in}} \times N \times F}$, Scaled Graph Laplacian $\tilde{L} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$.}
\KwOut{Multi-horizon category-specific forecast $\hat{\mathcal{Y}} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times H \times N \times 2}$}
\tcp{Initial Feature Permutation and Feature Scaling}
$\mathcal{H} \leftarrow \text{Permute}(\mathcal{X}) \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times F \times N \times T_{\text{in}}}$\;
\tcp{First ST-Conv Block ($C=80$ hidden channels)}
$\mathcal{H}_{\text{tmp1}} \leftarrow \text{GLU\_TemporalConv}_{1 \times K_t}(\mathcal{H})$ \tcp*{$\mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times N \times T_{\text{in}}}$}
$\mathcal{H}_{\text{sp1}} \leftarrow \text{ChebConv}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{tmp1}}, \tilde{L})$ \tcp*{$K=3$ Chebyshev order}
$\mathcal{H}_1 \leftarrow \text{GLU\_TemporalConv}_{1 \times K_t}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{sp1}}) + \mathcal{H}_{\text{res1}}$ \tcp*{$\mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times N \times T_{\text{in}}}$}

\tcp{Temporal Self-Attention Module ($h=4$ heads, Middle Position)}
Reshape $\mathcal{H}_1$ to $\mathcal{H}_{\text{seq}} \in \mathbb{R}^{(B \times N) \times T_{\text{in}} \times C}$\;
$\mathbf{Q} \leftarrow \mathcal{H}_{\text{seq}}\mathbf{W}_Q, \quad \mathbf{K} \leftarrow \mathcal{H}_{\text{seq}}\mathbf{W}_K, \quad \mathbf{V} \leftarrow \mathcal{H}_{\text{seq}}\mathbf{W}_V$\;
$\boldsymbol{\alpha} \leftarrow \text{Softmax}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)$ \tcp*{Pairwise temporal affinity}
$\mathcal{H}_{\text{attn}} \leftarrow \text{MultiHeadAttn}(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}) + \mathcal{H}_{\text{seq}}$ \tcp*{Residual connection}
Reshape $\mathcal{H}_{\text{attn}}$ back to $\mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times N \times T_{\text{in}}}$\;

\tcp{Second ST-Conv Block ($C=80$ hidden channels)}
$\mathcal{H}_{\text{tmp2}} \leftarrow \text{GLU\_TemporalConv}_{1 \times K_t}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{attn}})$ \tcp*{$\mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times N \times T_{\text{in}}}$}
$\mathcal{H}_{\text{sp2}} \leftarrow \text{ChebConv}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{tmp2}}, \tilde{L})$ \tcp*{$K=3$ Chebyshev order}
$\mathcal{H}_2 \leftarrow \text{GLU\_TemporalConv}_{1 \times K_t}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{sp2}}) + \mathcal{H}_{\text{res2}}$ \tcp*{$\mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times N \times T_{\text{in}}}$}

\tcp{Output Projection}
Reshape $\mathcal{H}_2$ to $\mathcal{H}_{\text{out}} \in \mathbb{R}^{(B \times N) \times C \times T_{\text{in}}}$\;
$\hat{\mathcal{Y}}_{\text{flat}} \leftarrow \text{Conv1D}_{K_t=T_{\text{in}}}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{out}})$ \tcp*{Project to $(H \times 2)$ channels}
Reshape $\hat{\mathcal{Y}}_{\text{flat}}$ to $\hat{\mathcal{Y}} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times H \times N \times 2}$ \tcp*{$H=6$ horizons, 2 categories}
\Return $\hat{\mathcal{Y}}$\;
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Camera Network Acquisition}
We harvest surveillance camera feeds and topological metadata from the public Ho Chi Minh City Traffic Portal via open civic access provided for public transportation monitoring and non-commercial scientific research. To establish the spatial graph nodes, we deploy an automated pipeline using standard client-side session management to parse public camera locations and extract geographical coordinates (Latitude, Longitude) for each camera station. All visual feeds are publicly accessible snapshots, processed strictly for non-commercial academic research without recording any personally identifiable information. 

For continuous visual perception, an automated multi-threaded API downloader with HTTP session pooling is deployed to query the image endpoints. To ensure strict temporal synchronization across the network, the downloader operates on a rigid cyclical schedule, scanning all initial 657 cameras exactly every 60 seconds. The acquired raw images are automatically organized into a time-aware hierarchical directory structure to facilitate efficient batch processing. After rigorously filtering out transient API connection failures, "Image not available" error payloads, and consistently offline feeds, \textbf{608 active camera stations} are established as the final graph node set $\mathcal{V}$ ($N = |\mathcal{V}| = 608$).

An automated multi-threaded API downloader with HTTP session pooling scans all 608 cameras every 60 seconds, building a synchronized, time-aligned sequence of traffic images. Figure~\ref{fig:api_samples} shows representative traffic camera feeds collected from the Ho Chi Minh City Traffic Portal API across major urban intersections. \begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Multi-Threaded Camera API Harvesting and Data Imputation}
\label{algo:api_harvesting}
\DontPrintSemicolon
\KwIn{Set of camera URLs $\mathcal{U} = \{u_1, \dots, u_N\}$ ($N=608$), Fetch interval $\Delta t = 1$ min, Max threads $M=32$.}
\KwOut{Synchronized vehicle count dataset $\mathcal{D}$}

$\mathcal{D} \leftarrow \emptyset$\;

}

\paragraph{Stage 1: Vision Dataset}
The camera vehicle estimation dataset consists of \textbf{5,012 manually annotated traffic images} captured comprehensively from an initial pool of \textbf{657 surveillance cameras} across the Ho Chi Minh City Traffic Portal. The data collection spanned from June 23, 2025, to September 16, 2025, capturing diverse lighting and traffic conditions throughout the day. To prevent the vision model from memorizing static camera background geometry, camera locations in the Validation (501 images) and Testing (501 images) sets are partitioned across disjoint intersections from those in the Training set (4,010 images), evaluating visual generalization under unseen camera perspectives. 

\textbf{Vehicle Density Distribution.} Statistical analysis of the annotations reveals an average of 30.28 vehicles per image ($\sigma = 25.07$), with a median of 24.00. The data exhibits a distinct right-skewed distribution, ranging from completely empty streets (0 vehicles) to instances of extreme congestion (up to 194 vehicles in a single frame). Categorically, motorcycles heavily dominate the traffic flow (mean: 23.02, max: 178) compared to passenger cars (mean: 7.26, max: 50). This distribution underscores the prevalence of moderate traffic alongside critical, hyper-dense edge cases.

\textbf{Addressing Dataset Limitations and Generalization.} A critical constraint of this dataset is the reliance on 4,010 training images to represent the immense visual diversity of 608 distinct camera perspectives, backgrounds, and varying lighting conditions. For a continuous counting regression task utilizing heavy feature extractors—such as Vision Transformer backbones containing approximately 28 million parameters—this limited sample size presents a significant challenge for spatial generalization. This constraint fundamentally explains why data-hungry architectures like Transformers may underperform or struggle to generalize compared to traditional convolutional networks in this specific configuration, as they lack the inductive biases necessary to learn efficiently from small datasets.

To mitigate this limitation and expand visual diversity without incurring additional manual annotation costs, specialized traffic-scene data augmentations are essential. These strategies include targeted random cropping of dense vehicle regions—forcing the model to learn localized vehicle features rather than relying on global spatial priors—and simulating artificial congestion to oversample the long-tail distribution of high-density scenarios.

To verify ground-truth labeling consistency across these dense vehicle clusters while managing manual annotation costs, a statistically significant random subset of 500 images (approximately 10\% of the dataset) was selected for cross-verification. This subset was independently annotated by three trained human annotators to establish baseline consistency before distributing the remaining workload. Inter-annotator reliability was quantified using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient ($\text{ICC}(3,k)$ two-way mixed-effects model for absolute agreement). The evaluation yielded an $\text{ICC} = 0.984$ for passenger car counts and $\text{ICC} = 0.962$ for motorcycle counts (overall combined $\text{ICC} = 0.973, p < 0.01$), confirming high general annotation consistency ($\text{ICC} > 0.90$). 

However, stratifying the analysis by traffic density reveals distinct challenges in edge cases. Under low-density conditions, annotator agreement remains near-perfect ($\text{ICC} > 0.99$). Conversely, under extreme congestion and severe partial occlusions (e.g., dense clusters of motorcycles weaving at signalized intersections), the motorcycle ICC drops to $0.941$, reflecting the inherent visual ambiguity human annotators face in highly unstructured mixed traffic. Following annotation, all images are resized to $224 \times 224$ pixels and normalized with ImageNet mean and standard deviation.

\paragraph{Stage 2: Traffic Graph Time-Series Dataset}
The data for Stage 2 is derived from the vision predictions generated in Stage 1. Initially, data was collected from a pool of 657 surveillance cameras. However, to ensure the integrity of the time-series forecasting, 49 cameras were excluded due to excessive missing timestamps that could not be reliably interpolated. Consequently, the remaining 608 active camera nodes form the spatial basis of the forecasting graph. 

The raw prediction outputs are first processed at a 1-minute temporal resolution. Due to occasional camera latency and system interruptions, there are missing timestamps within the data streams. To reconstruct a continuous time-series, a complete chronological timeline at a 1-minute frequency is generated for each camera node, spanning from its earliest to its latest recording. Missing timestamps are identified and filled using time-based linear interpolation (\textit{method="time"}), ensuring that brief gaps are smoothly bridged based on adjacent traffic states. Any remaining missing values (e.g., at the very edges of the timeline) are handled via backward and forward filling, and the resulting continuous counts are rounded to the nearest integer.

Following this 1-minute level reconstruction, the time series are aggregated into 5-minute intervals ($step\_minutes=5$) to form the final input sequences. These aggregated sequences are further smoothed via a double-rolling transformation ($window_1=3$, $window_2=5$) to mitigate high-frequency noise inherent in raw traffic counts.

\textbf{Per-Node Z-Score Normalization.} To ensure stable gradient propagation and eliminate scale bias across heterogeneous camera locations (where high-traffic intersections may observe $>100$ vehicles while residential streets observe $<5$), all vehicle count time-series are standardized using per-node Z-score normalization:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{v}_{i,t} = \frac{v_{i,t} - \mu_i}{\sigma_i + \epsilon}, \quad \epsilon = 10^{-6},
\end{equation}
where $\mu_i$ and $\sigma_i$ denote the per-node temporal mean and standard deviation computed \emph{strictly on the Training split}. 

\textbf{Strict Chronological Splitting.} To rigorously evaluate forecasting capability on future unseen data, the time-series dataset is partitioned along the temporal axis into Train (80\%), Validation (10\%), and Test (10\%) splits. This partition strictly preserves chronological order without any randomized temporal shuffling. The testing phase operates exclusively on the final 10\% contiguous window of the timeline, guaranteeing that all forecasted ``future'' time steps ($t+1$ to $t+6$) occur chronologically after the training and validation horizons. Furthermore, all $\mu_i, \sigma_i$ normalization statistics and double-rolling moving averages are computed solely on the historical Training window and applied forward to the Validation and Test windows, strictly preventing any backward temporal lookahead or data leakage.

Historical lookback length is configured as $T_{\text{in}} = 24$ steps (120 minutes of history), and the target forecast horizon is $H = 6$ steps (30 minutes ahead).

\paragraph{Strict Two-Stage Disjoint Isolation and Zero-Leakage Protocol}
To guarantee strict experimental isolation and eliminate camera-level data leakage between the Stage 1 vision perception backbone and Stage 2 graph forecasting models:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Disjoint Camera Splits for Stage 1 Perception Training:} The Stage 1 vision models (ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B4, ViT-Small, ConvNeXt-Tiny) are trained strictly on traffic images harvested from the Stage 1 Training camera locations (4,010 images). Camera stations assigned to the Stage 1 Validation (501 images) and Testing (501 images) sets are strictly partitioned across disjoint intersections from those in the Training set, evaluating visual count regression under unseen camera perspectives.
    \item \textbf{Strict Stage 2 Graph Training Isolation:} Stage 2 graph forecasting models are trained on vehicle count time-series streams generated by Stage 1 over the Stage 2 Training split (first 80\% contiguous window). Stage 2 loss optimization strictly utilizes Stage 1 predictions from training camera locations over the training window.
    \item \textbf{Zero Temporal and Spatial Leakage Guarantee:} All final Stage 2 multi-horizon forecasting benchmarks on the Test set (final 10\% contiguous window) are evaluated on vehicle count streams generated by Stage 1 on unseen camera views. No test-set camera images or future time steps are ever accessible during Stage 1 model training or Stage 2 parameter optimization, guaranteeing zero spatial or temporal data leakage.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Baseline 1 (Standard STGCN):} The foundational convolutional STGNN introduced by Yu et al. \cite{stgcn} (IJCAI 2018), utilizing 1D temporal Gated Linear Units (GLU) and Chebyshev spectral graph convolutions ($K=3$) \cite{chebnet} without attention mechanisms.
    \item \textbf{Baseline 2 (GraphWaveNet):} Wu et al. \cite{wavenet} (IJCAI 2019), combining adaptive graph adjacency matrix learning with stacked 1D dilated causal temporal convolutions to capture dynamic spatial-temporal dependencies.
    \item \textbf{Baseline 3 (ASTGCN):} Guo et al. \cite{astgcn} (AAAI 2019), incorporating spatial and temporal attention mechanisms within Chebyshev graph convolution blocks to dynamically capture dynamic spatio-temporal correlations.
    \item \textbf{Baseline 4 (DSTAGNN):} Lan et al. \cite{dstagnn} (ICML 2022), a Dynamic Spatial-Temporal Aware Graph Neural Network introducing dynamic graph construction and spatial-temporal attention.
    \item \textbf{Baseline 5 (MegaCRN):} Jiang et al. \cite{megacrn} (AAAI 2023), a Meta-Graph Convolutional Recurrent Network capturing spatio-temporal dynamics via meta-learning node embeddings.
    \item \textbf{Baseline 6 (STAEformer):} Liu et al. \cite{staeformer} (CIKM 2023), applying Spatio-Temporal Adaptive Embeddings to Transformer encoder blocks.
    \item \textbf{Baseline 7 (iTransformer):} Liu et al. \cite{itransformer} (ICLR 2024), an Inverted Transformer architecture applying self-attention over individual variates/nodes and feed-forward networks across temporal steps.
    \item \textbf{TA-STGCN (Proposed / Ours):} Our proposed architecture coupling lightweight ST-Conv blocks \cite{stgcn} with Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention ($h=4$) \cite{attention} as described in Section~\ref{sec:graph_tastgcn}.

The architectural choices in Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison} highlight three key computational trade-offs:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Spatial Convolution Operators vs. Dynamic Embeddings:} Standard STGCN \cite{stgcn} and TA-STGCN utilize 3rd-order Chebyshev Spectral Polynomials ($K=3$) \cite{chebnet} over a physical road distance graph, capturing localized multi-hop spatial dependencies with linear spatial complexity. In contrast, recent SOTA models (DSTAGNN \cite{dstagnn}, MegaCRN \cite{megacrn}, STAEformer \cite{staeformer}) rely on dense dynamic graph soft-attention or high-dimensional embeddings, incurring high memory allocation during training on city-scale 608-node networks.
    \item \textbf{Temporal Sequence Modeling Mechanisms:} While 1D Gated Temporal Convolutions (GLU) \cite{stgcn} possess fixed local receptive fields ($K_t=3$), SOTA Transformer baselines (STAEformer \cite{staeformer}, DSTAGNN \cite{dstagnn}) apply global attention over historical sequences. TA-STGCN bridges this gap by augmenting lightweight ST-Conv blocks with a Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention module ($h=4$) \cite{attention}, dynamically weighing temporal correlations across the entire 120-minute history without quadratic spatial memory explosion.
    \item \textbf{Controlled Capacity Design:} TA-STGCN maintains a balanced parameter footprint ($453,052$ parameters). While it introduces a marginal 20.7\% parameter increase over the lightweight STGCN Baseline ($375,212$ parameters) due to the multi-head attention projection layers, it achieves a substantial 70.4\% parameter reduction compared to heavy baselines like ASTGCN ($1,532,566$ parameters) while offering superior noise robustness and training stability.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Training Hyperparameters}
All models across both sub-problems are implemented in PyTorch 2.x and trained hardware-accelerated on an \textbf{NVIDIA T4 GPU (16 GB VRAM)}.

The vision backbones (ResNet-50 \cite{resnet}, EfficientNet-B4 \cite{efficientnet}, ViT-Small \cite{vit}, and ConvNeXt-Tiny \cite{convnext}) with unified MLP regression heads are trained under the following setup:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stage 1 Optimizer:} AdamW optimizer with initial learning rate $\eta = 1 \times 10^{-4}$ and weight decay coefficient $1 \times 10^{-2}$.
    \item \textbf{Learning Rate Schedule:} Cosine Annealing Learning Rate Scheduler decaying down to $\eta_{\min} = 1 \times 10^{-6}$.
    \item \textbf{Epochs and Batch Size:} Models are trained for 100 epochs with a mini-batch size of $B = 32$.
    \item \textbf{Loss Function:} Smooth $L_1$ (Huber) Loss \cite{huber} with threshold $\beta = 1.0$.
    \item \textbf{Data Augmentations:} Random horizontal flips (probability $p=0.5$), color jittering ($\pm 15\%$ brightness, contrast, and saturation), and random spatial cropping resized to $224 \times 224$.
\end{itemize}

All spatio-temporal graph forecasting models (STGCN \cite{stgcn}, GraphWaveNet \cite{wavenet}, ASTGCN \cite{astgcn}, STAEformer \cite{staeformer}, MegaCRN \cite{megacrn}, DSTAGNN \cite{dstagnn}, iTransformer \cite{itransformer}, and TA-STGCN) as well as all Ablation Study variants are trained under the following standardized setup:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Standard Architectural Benchmarking Protocol:} To ensure a strictly fair comparison, all baseline models (STGCN, GraphWaveNet, ASTGCN, STAEformer, MegaCRN, DSTAGNN, and iTransformer) are trained using the exact same optimization protocol (Adam, 120 epochs, Huber Loss, Early Stopping). Only their internal architectural hyperparameters (e.g., hidden dimensions, number of layers) are instantiated following their canonical author-recommended configurations. TA-STGCN ($453,052$ parameters) achieves a balanced trade-off among parameter efficiency, GPU memory footprint, and multi-horizon forecasting precision (detailed memory analysis is provided in Section~\ref{sec:deployment_limitations}).
    \item \textbf{Hardware Infrastructure:} All graph experiments across benchmark baselines and ablation variants are executed hardware-accelerated on GPU infrastructure under PyTorch 2.x.
    \item \textbf{Stage 2 Optimizer:} Adam optimizer \cite{adam} with initial learning rate $\eta = 1 \times 10^{-3}$ and weight decay coefficient $1 \times 10^{-4}$.
    \item \textbf{Learning Rate Schedule:} \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} scheduler decaying the learning rate by a factor of $\gamma = 0.5$ if validation loss fails to decrease for 10 consecutive epochs ($\text{patience} = 10$).
    \item \textbf{Epochs and Early Stopping:} Trained for up to $N_{\text{max}} = 80$ epochs with an Early Stopping threshold of 10 epochs ($\text{patience} = 10$) monitoring validation loss to prevent overfitting and guarantee fair convergence across variants.
    \item \textbf{Batch Size:} Training mini-batch size $B = 32$ (evaluation mini-batch size $B = 32$).
    \item \textbf{Loss Function:} Huber Loss \cite{huber} ($\delta = 1.0$). The Huber loss acts as $L_2$ (MSE) for small errors and $L_1$ (MAE) for large errors. While MAE treats all errors equally and MSE overly penalizes extreme outliers (which are common due to Stage 1 perception noise during heavy occlusions), Huber loss provides a crucial variance-bias balance. By acting as MAE for large outlier errors, it prevents gradients from exploding in noisy camera conditions, thereby maintaining the high training stability ($\text{CV}=0.23\%$) observed in our experiments. The threshold $\delta = 1.0$ is chosen to align with the unit scale of the Z-score-normalized count residuals: after per-node standardization (Section~\ref{sec:datasets}), a residual of magnitude $|\hat{y} - y| = 1$ corresponds to exactly one standard deviation of the node's historical traffic distribution. Consequently, residuals below one standard deviation (the regime dominated by benign, small-scale perception jitter) are penalized quadratically to encourage smooth, precise convergence, whereas residuals exceeding one standard deviation (the regime dominated by severe occlusion-induced undercounting or transient camera dropouts) are penalized linearly to bound gradient magnitude and prevent a single noisy sample from dominating the update. This unit-aligned threshold therefore yields a principled, scale-invariant transition between the $L_2$ and $L_1$ regimes without requiring per-node tuning.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Seed Evaluation Protocol:} Evaluated across independent random seeds. All reported evaluation metrics present $\text{Mean} \pm \text{Std}$ across random seeds.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Evaluation Metrics}
% NOTE (Round 1): the separate MSE equation was removed as redundant
% (RMSE = sqrt(MSE)); the MSE row in the benchmark table was removed accordingly.
Model performance is evaluated using Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE):
\begin{equation}
\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{N \cdot H \cdot M} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{h=1}^{H} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \left| y_{i,h}^{(k)} - \hat{y}_{i,h}^{(k)} \right|,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{N \cdot H \cdot M} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{h=1}^{H} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \left( y_{i,h}^{(k)} - \hat{y}_{i,h}^{(k)} \right)^2 },
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{MAPE} = \frac{1}{|\Omega|} \sum_{(i,h,k) \in \Omega} \frac{\left| y_{i,h}^{(k)} - \hat{y}_{i,h}^{(k)} \right|}{y_{i,h}^{(k)} + \epsilon} \times 100\%,
\end{equation}
where $N=608$ graph nodes, $H=6$ forecast steps, $M$ is the test batch count, $\Omega = \{ (i,h,k) \mid y_{i,h}^{(k)} > 0.5 \}$ isolates non-zero traffic intervals, and $\epsilon = 10^{-5}$ prevents division instability during low-volume night-time steps.

\paragraph{Statistical vs. Practical Significance Analysis}
When evaluating model performance across massive spatio-temporal datasets ($N=608$ nodes, $H=6$ horizons, $M \approx 5,000$ test sequence windows, totaling over $1.8 \times 10^7$ prediction points), standard hypothesis tests (e.g., paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests) almost universally yield extreme statistical significance ($p < 0.001$). However, in large-sample empirical benchmarks, statistical significance ($p$-value) does not automatically translate to \emph{practical significance}.

\subsection{Robustness Analysis}
\label{sec:noise_robustness}

An inherent vulnerability of two-stage ITS architectures is \emph{two-stage error propagation}: because high-precision inductive loop sensors are absent across city-scale camera networks, Stage 2 graph forecasting models are trained on vehicle count sequences produced by Stage 1 vision perception backbones (which carry intrinsic visual estimation noise).

To rigorously evaluate how Stage 1 perception noise cascades into Stage 2 graph forecasting, we conduct a \textbf{Noise Sensitivity Analysis}. Rather than relying on arbitrary synthetic Gaussian noise, the noise injection parameters and evaluation levels ($\Delta \text{MAE}_{\text{noise}}$) are \textbf{strictly derived from the empirical error statistics of the Stage 1 vision models} evaluated across our 501 test camera feeds.

Specifically, empirical evaluation of Stage 1 backbones (Table~\ref{tab:stage1_density}) reveals two fundamental physical characteristics of camera-based vehicle counting under dense mixed traffic:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Asymmetric Undercounting Bias:} Under heavy congestion, severe inter-vehicle occlusions (motorcycles overlapping passenger cars) consistently bias the visual backbones toward \emph{undercounting} (as reflected by negative prediction bias across dense strata in Table~\ref{tab:stage1_density}).
    \item \textbf{Heteroscedastic Scaling:} Absolute counting error magnitudes scale proportionally with local node volume (higher vehicle density inherently incurs larger absolute error bounds).
\end{enumerate}

To capture these empirical properties, we model Stage 1 perception noise using a \emph{Heteroscedastic Asymmetric} Gamma process, where 80\% of injected noise terms are negative (matching the empirical undercounting bias) and the noise scale is proportional to node volume. Crucially, the target evaluation levels ($\Delta \text{MAE}_{\text{noise}}$) correspond directly to the empirical perception error distribution generated across individual test cameras in Stage 1:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Level 1 ($\Delta \text{MAE} = 3.49$):} Grounded in the minimum empirical camera counting error ($\text{MAE}_{\min} = 3.488 \approx 3.49$), representing optimal camera angles and unoccluded off-peak feeds.
    \item \textbf{Level 2 ($\Delta \text{MAE} = 4.64$):} Grounded in the mean empirical perception error ($\text{MAE}_{\text{mean}} = 4.640 \approx 4.64$) averaged across operational traffic camera conditions.
    \item \textbf{Level 3 ($\Delta \text{MAE} = 10.25$):} Grounded in the maximum empirical error bound ($\text{MAE}_{\max} = 10.250 \approx 10.25$) recorded at severely occluded, high-density rush-hour intersections (aligning with the $9.76 - 12.26$ motorcycle MAE observed under High density in Table~\ref{tab:stage1_density}).
    \item \textbf{Level 4 ($\Delta \text{MAE} = 13.95$):} Simulates extreme perception breakdown under compound adverse conditions ($\text{MAE}_{\max} + 2\sigma_{\text{MAE}} = 10.25 + 2 \times 1.849 \approx 13.95$).
\end{itemize}

When camera count inputs experience transient perception noise, multi-head temporal self-attention down-weights noisy high-frequency sequence spikes and aggregates consistent temporal momentum across the 120-minute window, effectively dampening error propagation in two-stage ITS pipelines.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:deployment_limitations}

\subsection{Envisioned Conceptual Edge-Cloud Architecture}
\label{sec:deployment_arch}

Deploying a unified spatio-temporal forecasting framework across a city-scale network of 608 high-resolution surveillance cameras introduces substantial infrastructural bottlenecks. To ensure scalability without overwhelming network bandwidth, we envision a conceptual decoupled Edge-Cloud architecture:

\paragraph{Edge-Level Visual Perception (Stage 1)} Raw video streams are processed locally via EfficientNet-B4 on edge devices using optimized runtimes (e.g., OpenVINO, NCNN) and mixed-precision quantization, following edge intelligence principles \cite{edge_intelligence, edge_its_survey}. The edge nodes emit only lightweight structured payloads (vehicle counts), preserving network bandwidth and public privacy. For instance, rather than transmitting a standard 1080p camera snapshot ($\sim 500$ KB) over municipal networks, the edge node transmits only a 2-dimensional count vector $[\hat{c}_{\text{car}}, \hat{c}_{\text{bike}}]^T$ ($8$ bytes), theoretically reducing network bandwidth requirements by over $60,000\times$ and eliminating transmission bottlenecks.

\paragraph{Distributed Cloud Forecasting (Stage 2)} The count payloads are ingested centrally via message brokers (e.g., Apache Kafka). TA-STGCN forecasting modules, orchestrated via Kubernetes, then execute multi-horizon network-wide congestion predictions asynchronously.

\subsection{Parameter Economy vs. GPU Activation Memory}

A key hardware design insight from our benchmarks (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_5seeds}) is that static parameter count ($N_{\text{params}}$) alone is insufficient to evaluate model efficiency for city-scale graph architectures. While SOTA models like STAEformer maintain moderate parameter footprints ($\sim 568\text{K}$), their internal dynamic soft-attention allocations generate large activation footprints across $N=608$ nodes (\textbf{11,509.1 MB} peak GPU memory).

In contrast, TA-STGCN explicitly decouples spatial Chebyshev graph convolutions from temporal self-attention. While this increases peak GPU memory by $77.9\%$ over the naive STGCN baseline ($4,837.6 \to 8,606.9$ MB), it still achieves a \textbf{25.2\% reduction} compared to STAEformer ($11,509.1$ MB). Furthermore, TA-STGCN achieves the lowest overall forecasting error ($\text{MAE} = \mathbf{3.2401 \pm 0.0108}$) with a \textbf{70.4\% parameter reduction} over heavy baselines like ASTGCN ($1,533\text{K} \to 453\text{K}$), establishing a competitive trade-off among parameter capacity, activation memory footprint, and multi-horizon forecasting accuracy for cost-constrained real-world deployment.

As Table~\ref{tab:total_cost} makes explicit, the total system encompasses over \textbf{17.2 billion parameters} across the 608-node camera network when deploying EfficientNet-B4 per edge node. This asymmetry carries three practical implications for municipal deployment:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Edge Inference Dominates Hardware Budget:} Each edge node must run a 28.3M-parameter EfficientNet-B4 at 75.3 GFLOPs per frame. While optimized runtimes (OpenVINO, NCNN) and INT8 quantization can reduce this to $\sim$8--10 GFLOPs, the aggregate edge computational load across 608 nodes remains substantial. In contrast, the cloud-side TA-STGCN (453K parameters) requires negligible compute per inference step, enabling cost-effective centralized deployment on Kubernetes.
    \item \textbf{Communication Asymmetry Favors the Architecture:} The extreme bandwidth reduction ($60,000\times$) from raw images (500 KB) to count vectors (8 bytes) at the edge means that even with 608 nodes transmitting every 5 minutes, the total cloud ingress is only $\sim$1.3 KB/s -- well within the capacity of a single municipal broadband connection. This asymmetric design eliminates the primary network bottleneck in city-scale camera deployments.
    \item \textbf{Future Optimization Pathway:} The dominant cost driver is the per-camera vision backbone. Knowledge distillation from EfficientNet-B4 to MobileNet-V3 (4.2M parameters, 6.9 GFLOPs) would reduce the total system footprint from 17.2B to 2.6B parameters -- a $85\%$ reduction -- while incurring only a modest MAE increase of $+0.3763$ ($3.6363 \to 4.0126$). For municipalities operating under stringent edge hardware constraints, this trade-off offers a viable deployment alternative.
\end{enumerate}

These observations reinforce that TA-STGCN's parameter economy is not merely an academic metric but a practical necessity for the two-stage architecture: the cloud forecasting model must remain lightweight to accommodate the dominant perceptual computational load at the edge.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
Several limitations remain in this study. First, the Stage 2 models are trained on vehicle count pseudo-labels produced by Stage 1, making forecasting fidelity inherently bound by visual perception error margins. Second, the total system computational cost is substantial. While the Stage 2 graph model is lightweight (453K parameters), Stage 1 requires deploying a 28.3M-parameter EfficientNet-B4 per camera. Across a 608-node network, this entails over $17$ billion total parameters, posing significant Edge deployment challenges that require future optimization via INT8 quantization or knowledge distillation to smaller backbones (e.g., MobileNet). Third, the Stage 1 dataset contains only 5,012 images, which may limit generalizability across the 608 cameras; future studies should employ semi-supervised learning or data augmentation. Fourth, while TA-STGCN preserves vehicle categories by predicting multi-channel outputs ($C=2$), it processes these channels independently and lacks a cross-attention mechanism to explicitly model the micro-interactions between cars and motorcycles (e.g., motorcycles weaving between cars during congestion). Fifth, the evaluation is constrained to a single city (Ho Chi Minh City); future work must investigate zero-shot model generalization to other motorcycle-dominant metropolises (e.g., Bangkok, Jakarta). Sixth, the dataset spans a 3-month temporal window (June--September 2025), which lacks extreme seasonal or weather variations (e.g., monsoon flooding) that significantly alter traffic flow. Seventh, while edge runtimes are proposed for Stage 1 deployment, empirical hardware benchmarking for latency and power consumption on actual edge devices was left for future work. Finally, the static physical graph topology (RBF adjacency matrix) cannot dynamically adapt to real-time structural shifts in traffic flows caused by unpredictable road incidents. Future work will explore dynamic graph structure learning \cite{staggcn, dstagnn} or neural controlled differential equations \cite{stgncde} to adaptively capture these non-stationary spatial dependencies.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper introduced an end-to-end, privacy-preserving framework for urban traffic monitoring and multi-horizon forecasting, evaluated on a real-world 608-node camera network in Ho Chi Minh City. By decoupling the pipeline into edge-level vehicle perception (EfficientNet-B4) and cloud-level graph forecasting (TA-STGCN), the system addresses the unique challenges of heterogeneous, motorcycle-dominant mixed traffic without relying on expensive inductive loops. Our contributions span three fronts: (i) a two-stage camera-sensed framework that bridges category-preserving direct-regression perception with network-wide spatio-temporal graph forecasting; (ii) a large-scale, camera-only 608-node traffic benchmark with a static OSM-RBF physical adjacency matrix that anchors message passing along physically permissible flow paths; and (iii) \emph{TA-STGCN}, a hybrid architecture coupling Chebyshev spectral graph convolutions with model-level multi-head temporal self-attention ($h=4$) that acts as an adaptive dynamic filter for two-stage perception noise.

Comprehensive multi-seed evaluations demonstrate that TA-STGCN achieves consistent point-forecasting accuracy ($\text{MAE} = \mathbf{3.2401 \pm 0.0108}$, $\text{MAPE} = 11.32\%$) while operating with exceptional stability across random initializations ($\text{CV} = 0.23\%$). Its primary architectural value, however, lies in operational robustness: under extreme Stage 1 perception noise, TA-STGCN reduces forecasting error by up to $4.95$ MAE units ($32.8\%$) relative to GraphWaveNet, and it bounds long-horizon error accumulation more effectively than all baselines. Ablation studies confirm that these gains stem from the temporal self-attention inductive bias rather than parameter scaling, and that the $h=4$ configuration optimally balances subspace diversity against computational overhead. Furthermore, TA-STGCN maintains a compact 453K-parameter footprint with $25.2\%$ lower peak memory than Transformer baselines, making it well-suited for resource-constrained Edge-Cloud deployments.

Several directions remain for future work. First, the total system cost is dominated by the per-camera vision backbone (17.2B aggregate parameters); knowledge distillation to lighter backbones (e.g., MobileNet) and INT8 quantization offer a promising path to reduce this footprint by up to $85\%$. Second, extending the framework to cross-attention between vehicle categories, dynamic graph structure learning, and zero-shot generalization to other motorcycle-dominant metropolises would broaden its applicability. Finally, expanding the dataset across seasonal and weather variations would strengthen the generalizability of the proposed framework to diverse operational conditions.

\section*{Ethics and Visual Privacy Statement}
All traffic surveillance camera feeds analyzed in this study were obtained from the public Ho Chi Minh City Traffic Portal via open civic access provided for public transportation monitoring and non-commercial scientific research. Protecting public privacy and adhering to ethical surveillance standards are central to the design of this framework. To ensure strict anonymization, our envisioned Edge-Cloud architecture confines all visual processing directly to the edge (Stage 1). The edge nodes extract only aggregated numerical payloads (category-specific vehicle counts). Consequently, no raw video frames, license plates, or human facial details are ever stored, logged, or transmitted to the centralized cloud servers. Furthermore, the inherent camera mounting elevations, long observation distances, and stream resolution limits render individual personally identifiable information (PII) fundamentally non-identifiable. We acknowledge that large-scale camera monitoring runs the risk of normalizing public surveillance; hence, by designing a system that mathematically destroys PII at the source, this work advocates for privacy-by-design in municipal sensing.
