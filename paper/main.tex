\begin{abstract}
While surveillance camera networks offer high-coverage visual feeds, few benchmarks jointly evaluate camera-level vehicle perception and network-wide forecasting under motorcycle-dominant mixed traffic. Our primary contribution is a camera-network benchmark of 608 nodes in Ho Chi Minh City, on which we evaluate a two-stage perception-and-forecasting pipeline and a set of representative graph and Transformer baselines. Stage 1 uses EfficientNet-B5 for direct category-specific vehicle-count regression; Stage 2 evaluates \emph{TA-STGCN}, a reference adaptation of STGCN that inserts temporal self-attention between its ST-Conv blocks. On clean inputs the models are practically equivalent in point accuracy (TA-STGCN $\text{MAE} = 3.2401 \pm 0.0108$, a $\sim$0.9\% gain over the STGCN baseline, Cohen's $d = -0.032$); we therefore do not claim a clean-input accuracy advance. Under simulated camera noise the effect \emph{stratifies by competitor class}: TA-STGCN is substantially more robust than the non-attention graph/RNN baselines (e.g., Cohen's $d = +3.65$ vs.\ its STGCN predecessor, $+5.84$ vs.\ GraphWaveNet at Level 4), while against the attention-based baselines (STAEformer, iTransformer) its margin is only small-to-medium ($d \in [0.28, 0.67]$) and not a decisive separation. The honest reading is that attention-based temporal modeling is robust to perception noise on this benchmark, and TA-STGCN is one parameter-economical realization (453K parameters, $25.2\%$ less peak training memory than STAEformer)---not that it dominates the SOTA attention baselines. An edge-cloud deployment that transmits counts rather than images is presented as a prospective architecture; its device-level latency, energy use, and privacy guarantees remain to be validated in deployment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Rapid global urbanization and the surging number of personal vehicles—especially passenger cars and motorcycles—cause severe traffic congestion in fast-growing metropolitan areas like Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. Since expanding physical road infrastructure is prohibitively slow and expensive, Artificial Intelligence (AI) augmented Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS) emerge as a cost-effective solution for real-time traffic perception and proactive congestion forecasting.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig/traffic_jam.jpg}
\caption{Urban traffic congestion in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. This scene illustrates the motorcycle-dominant mixed traffic challenge, characterized by highly unstructured lateral movements, lack of strict lane discipline, and severe visual occlusions between passenger cars and motorcycles.}
\label{fig:traffic_jam}
\end{figure}

In modern urban environments, surveillance camera networks offer high-coverage visual feeds at lower cost than physical induction loop sensors. Transforming raw visual camera feeds into proactive city-wide traffic intelligence requires two sequential stages: (1) \textbf{Camera-Level Perception} (estimating category-specific vehicle counts from individual feeds) and (2) \textbf{Network-Wide Forecasting} (forecasting traffic flow trends across the interconnected road graph). Independent single-node forecasting is inadequate because congestion propagates to connected downstream road segments \cite{ctm}.

To address Stage 2, we adapt the efficient STGCN \cite{stgcn} into \textbf{TA-STGCN} (Temporal Attention-Guided Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network) by inserting a Temporal Self-Attention module \cite{attention} between its two ST-Conv blocks. This is an architectural adaptation rather than a claim of a wholly new attention mechanism. Its role is evaluated empirically as a way to reweight temporal context in the presence of two-stage perception errors; the paper's primary contribution is the camera-sensed benchmark and its end-to-end robustness analysis.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows, in descending order of weight. The leading contributions are the benchmark and the two-stage error analysis; \emph{TA-STGCN is positioned as a reference architecture evaluated on that benchmark, not as the central methodological claim}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{City-Scale Camera-Only Mixed-Traffic Benchmark (Primary Contribution):} To address the pronounced scarcity of empirical datasets capturing chaotic mixed-traffic dynamics in developing nations, this work establishes a large-scale 608-node city-scale traffic forecasting benchmark derived entirely from municipal camera infrastructure. The graph is constructed via automated multi-threaded API pipelines harvesting data from 657 CCTV cameras, integrated with topological road distances and RBF-weighted adjacency matrices. To our knowledge this is the first openly described camera-sensed, category-preserving, city-scale benchmark for a motorcycle-dominant metropolis, and its intended use is as a shared testbed on which future architectures---including more recent state-space and patch-based forecasters---can be compared under matched camera-sensed conditions.
    \item \textbf{Camera-Sensed Two-Stage Framework and Annotated Dataset:} We design a two-stage ITS framework that bridges camera-level visual perception (Stage 1) with network-wide spatio-temporal graph forecasting (Stage 2), addressing motorcycle-dominant mixed traffic without inductive loops, and we release a real-world dataset of 5,012 manually annotated traffic camera images (4,010 Train, 501 Validation, 501 Test) with continuous category-specific count annotations.
    \item \textbf{Two-Stage Error-Cascade and Noise-Robustness Analysis:} We benchmark seven representative graph/Transformer baselines plus a reference adaptation (\emph{TA-STGCN}) across 5 independent random seeds using point accuracy, noise sensitivity, variance, and hardware efficiency. The analysis characterizes how Stage 1 perception error cascades into Stage 2 forecasting, and reports effect sizes (Cohen's $d$) rather than $p$-values given the large evaluation sample. A prospective edge-cloud architecture is discussed separately from the centrally executed experimental pipeline.
    \item \textbf{TA-STGCN as a Reference Adaptation (Secondary):} As one architecture evaluated on the benchmark, we adapt STGCN \cite{stgcn} by inserting a Model-Level Temporal Self-Attention module \cite{attention} between its two ST-Conv blocks. We frame this as a design-point study, not a new attention mechanism: on clean inputs the module yields only a $\sim$0.9\% relative MAE improvement over the STGCN baseline (Cohen's $d = -0.032$, practically equivalent), so it is \emph{not} sold on clean-input accuracy. Its role in the paper is to show that a near-free clean-input adaptation can convert into a large robustness effect under camera-sensed noise (Cohen's $d$ up to $+3.65$ vs.\ the STGCN predecessor at Level 4), illustrating the kind of robustness--cost trade-off the benchmark is meant to expose.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the datasets, the two-stage methodology, and the experimental configuration. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the experimental results. Section~\ref{sec:deployment_limitations} discusses the implications, hardware efficiency, and deployment limitations. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\paragraph{Scope of Claims}
We bound our claims to what the reported experiments support. The temporal-attention module is an architectural adaptation of STGCN, not a new attention mechanism; its value is characterized empirically as a robustness gain over its predecessor under camera-sensed noise, not asserted as a theoretical guarantee. The static physical graph is a design choice tested under that noise, not a claim of universal superiority over adaptive graphs. The conditional-variance identity in Section~\ref{sec:methods} motivates the module's capacity to temper perturbations; the actual denoising behavior is verified empirically. The edge-cloud architecture is prospective; its latency, energy, and privacy properties are future-work validation targets, not measured results. Where a claim touches these boundaries we state the limitation inline rather than re-listing it.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Building city-scale traffic intelligence from camera networks requires progress on two coupled fronts: extracting reliable vehicle measurements from visual feeds, and forecasting their spatio-temporal evolution. However, most existing literature addresses these problems under conditions that differ sharply from the camera-sensed, motorcycle-dominant urban traffic this study targets. We summarize this gap in Table~\ref{tab:related_comparison}.

\subsection{Visual Vehicle Counting Under Dense Traffic}
Visual vehicle counting traditionally relies on Tracking-by-Detection pipelines (e.g., YOLO + ByteTrack \cite{yolo}), density-map estimation (e.g., MCNN \cite{mcnn}, CSRNet \cite{csrnet}, CAN \cite{can}), or point-supervised counters such as P2PNet and DM-Count. The latter two families are effective in dense scenes, but require point or density supervision and careful camera-specific annotation and calibration. Producing the required point-level or density-map ground truth for a city-scale, class-separated dataset is substantially more expensive than the aggregate count labels used here: each image must be annotated with per-vehicle dots or a per-pixel density map, and the counters must be re-calibrated per camera viewpoint, multiplying labeling and deployment cost across hundreds of nodes. We therefore position direct regression as a practical choice for this dataset, not as a universal replacement for specialized counting methods. The prevailing alternatives are ill-suited for the targeted deployment for three reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Extreme Mutual Occlusion:} In developing nations, motorcycles do not adhere to strict lane discipline, resulting in severe physical occlusion during congestion (in our dataset, dense clusters frequently exhibit 80--90\% of vehicles partially or fully occluded in a single frame). Bounding boxes overlap catastrophically, triggering severe Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) bottlenecks that blind standard object detectors.
    \item \textbf{Temporal Discontinuity:} Public municipal camera APIs typically provide static image snapshots at low polling frequencies (e.g., 1 frame per minute) to conserve bandwidth. This severe temporal sparsity breaks the fundamental continuous-frame assumption required for Tracking-by-Detection algorithms to function.
    \item \textbf{Edge Hardware Constraints:} Running heavy multi-object tracking pipelines per camera node is economically prohibitive for city-scale deployments. Density maps are computationally lighter but inherently class-agnostic, destroying the vital car-vs-motorcycle distinction.
\end{enumerate}

To bypass these limitations, we adopt a \textbf{Direct Regression} approach. We map static camera images directly to continuous, category-preserving vehicle counts: $I \to [\hat{c}_{\text{car}}, \hat{c}_{\text{bike}}]^T$. While direct regression can overfit global background contexts (e.g., lighting or road geometry), our qualitative feature-attribution analysis (via Grad-CAM++ in Section~\ref{sec:results}) indicates that the selected backbone concentrates activations on active vehicle clusters. A direct comparison with point- or density-supervised counters is left to future work when matched point annotations and camera calibration are available; the aggregate count labels used here avoid the substantial per-vehicle labeling and per-camera calibration cost that such specialized counters would require.

\subsection{Spatio-Temporal Traffic Flow Forecasting}
Traffic forecasting has evolved from traditional statistical baselines (e.g., ARIMA \cite{arima}, SVR \cite{svr}) to sequential RNNs (e.g., DCRNN \cite{dcrnn}, T-GCN \cite{tgcn}, GC-LSTM \cite{gclstm}), spectral GCNs (e.g., Kipf \& Welling \cite{gcn}), and modern STGNN architectures (e.g., STGCN \cite{stgcn}, GraphWaveNet \cite{wavenet}, STID \cite{stid}, TPA-LSTM \cite{tpalstm}, ST-GNCDE \cite{stgncde}). Detailed taxonomies of spatio-temporal deep learning paradigms are provided in comprehensive surveys \cite{yin_survey, gnn_survey}. While convolutional models are highly efficient, their localized 1D temporal receptive fields struggle with non-stationary long-range dependencies. Conversely, pure self-attention Transformers (e.g., ASTGCN \cite{astgcn}, STAEformer \cite{staeformer}) achieve high accuracy but incur prohibitive quadratic spatial-temporal memory footprints across large urban graphs. In this work, we benchmark against a representative set spanning both paradigms---the convolutional STGCN \cite{stgcn} and GraphWaveNet \cite{wavenet}, the attention-augmented ASTGCN \cite{astgcn} and DSTAGNN \cite{dstagnn}, the meta-learning MegaCRN \cite{megacrn}, and the Transformer-based STAEformer \cite{staeformer} and iTransformer \cite{itransformer}---to situate our proposed architecture within this landscape.

\subsection{Hybrid Architectures and Adaptive Graphs}
To bridge the gap between convolutional efficiency and transformer accuracy, recent hybrid architectures explore mixing CNNs and Transformers (e.g., PDFormer \cite{pdformer}). Additionally, recognizing the limitations of static distance-based graphs, modern STGNNs increasingly utilize dynamic or adaptive adjacency matrices to capture shifting correlations (e.g., STAG-GCN \cite{staggcn}, STJGCN \cite{stjgcn}). However, these methods often remain computationally expensive. TA-STGCN contributes to this hybrid paradigm by explicitly decoupling spatial Chebyshev graph convolutions from temporal self-attention, coupling the efficiency of convolutional ST-Conv blocks with a global temporal receptive field. An edge implementation that transmits only aggregate count payloads is a prospective privacy-oriented design; the experiments in this study instead use centrally retrieved image snapshots.

\subsection{Commercial Systems and Camera-Based Infrastructure}
While commercial platforms like Uber Movement and Google Maps provide real-time congestion data, their proprietary routing algorithms and lack of granular volume metrics make them unsuitable for open municipal forecasting benchmarks. Recently, camera-to-forecast pipelines have gained traction (e.g., \cite{feng2024}, \cite{liu2024}), yet they primarily address structured, car-dominant environments. Similarly, while localized camera-based traffic studies exist in Southeast Asian metropolises such as Hanoi \cite{vu2026}, they primarily focus on isolated intersections rather than continuous, city-scale graph forecasting.

\subsection{The Key Challenge: Mixed, Motorcycle-Dominant Traffic}
\label{sec:gap}

The fundamental limitation of prevailing architectures is their development on highly structured, car-dominant highway benchmarks (e.g., METR-LA, PEMS-BAY) collected via induction loop detectors \cite{dcrnn, stgcn}. These datasets assume predictable fluid-dynamic flows and strict lane discipline.

In contrast, urban traffic in Southeast Asian metropolises is chaotic and motorcycle-dominant. As established in classical and modern traffic studies \cite{minh2003, cao2007, lee2009, mixedtraffic_stgnn}, vehicles cluster densely without lane adherence, causing highly non-stationary temporal fluctuations and sudden spatial spillovers. Because camera-sensed counts exhibit inherent transient perception noise during severe occlusions, applying rigid convolutional architectures directly to this data propagates estimation errors across multi-step forecasts.

As Table~\ref{tab:related_comparison} makes explicit, no existing benchmark addresses the combined challenge of camera-only sensing and chaotic mixed traffic at a network-wide scale, and none preserves per-category vehicle information beyond the perception stage. This study is positioned directly at that intersection: it couples category-preserving direct-regression perception with network-wide graph forecasting, evaluated end-to-end on a real, city-scale 608-node camera network in Ho Chi Minh City---establishing a uniquely large-scale testbed for a motorcycle-dominant metropolis---using a temporal-attention architecture whose parameter economy suits the modest computational budgets realistic for such deployments.

\subsection{Stage 1: Vehicle Counting}
\label{sec:subproblem1}

Stage 1 maps individual camera images to continuous vehicle counts per category $[\hat{c}_{\text{car}}, \hat{c}_{\text{bike}}]^T$. To identify the most suitable visual backbone for motorcycle-dominant traffic scenes, we benchmark five representative architectures: convolutional ResNet-50 \cite{resnet}, EfficientNet-B5 \cite{efficientnet}, ConvNeXt-Tiny \cite{convnext}, and MobileNet-V3 \cite{mobilenet}, as well as attention-based ViT-Base/16 \cite{vit}.

\subsubsection{ResNet-50 Baseline Backbone}
ResNet-50 \cite{resnet} is utilized as a standard baseline. We project the input image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 224 \times 224}$ into a 2048-dimensional feature representation via Global Average Pooling (GAP):
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{resnet}} = \text{GAP}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\text{ResNet50}}(I)\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{2048}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{EfficientNet-B5 Backbone}
EfficientNet-B5 \cite{efficientnet} applies compound scaling (depth, width, resolution) optimized via neural architecture search. We extract:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{eff}} = \text{GAP}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\text{EfficientNet\text{-}B5}}(I)\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{2048}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{ViT-Base/16 Backbone}
Vision Transformer Base (ViT-B/16) \cite{vit} tokenizes the input image into $16 \times 16$ patches and applies multi-head self-attention across the patch sequence. We extract the \texttt{[CLS]} token representation:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{vit}} = \mathcal{F}_{\text{ViT\text{-}B/16}}(I)_{\texttt{[CLS]}} \in \mathbb{R}^{768}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{ConvNeXt-Tiny Backbone}
ConvNeXt-Tiny \cite{convnext} modernizes pure convolutional designs with depthwise separable $7 \times 7$ convolutions, inverted bottleneck blocks, and LayerNorm:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{cnxt}} = \text{GAP}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\text{ConvNeXt}}(I)\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{768}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{MobileNet-V3 Backbone}
MobileNet-V3 \cite{mobilenet} is included as a compact deployment-oriented CNN baseline. It combines depthwise separable convolutions, squeeze-and-excitation blocks, and hardware-aware neural architecture search; its pooled feature vector is passed to the same regression head as the other backbones.

\subsubsection{Unified MLP Regression Head}
For all five backbones, the ImageNet classification layer is removed and replaced by a unified Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) regression head. Given a backbone feature vector $\mathbf{f} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ (where $d$ varies by architecture):
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_1 = \text{LayerNorm}\left( \mathbf{W}_1 \mathbf{f} + \mathbf{b}_1 \right), \quad \mathbf{W}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{128 \times d},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{c}} = \mathbf{W}_2 \cdot \text{ReLU}(\mathbf{h}_1) + \mathbf{b}_2, \quad \mathbf{W}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 128},
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mathbf{c}} = [\hat{c}_{\text{car}}, \hat{c}_{\text{bike}}]^T \in \mathbb{R}^2$ represents the estimated continuous counts of passenger cars and motorcycles in image $I$. Using a shared regression head isolates the effect of the visual backbone from the task-specific head, ensuring a fair comparison across architectures.

\subsubsection{Objective Function}
Stage 1 is trained end-to-end using the Smooth $L_1$ (Huber) Loss \cite{huber} to provide robustness against annotation noise in dense traffic scenes:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{vision}}(\mathbf{c}, \hat{\mathbf{c}}) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{m \in \{\text{car}, \text{bike}\}} \text{Smooth}_{L_1}\left(c_m - \hat{c}_m\right),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\text{Smooth}_{L_1}(x) = \begin{cases} 0.5 x^2, & \text{if } |x| < 1 \\ |x| - 0.5, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Stage 2: Traffic Graph and TA-STGCN}
\label{sec:stage2}
\label{sec:graph_tastgcn}

To capture spatial traffic spillover across the city, estimated vehicle counts across camera locations are integrated into an urban road network graph. It is worth noting that due to the time-series data preprocessing, the initial $657$ camera feeds were reduced to $608$ active nodes (the detailed preprocessing pipeline will be further discussed in Section~\ref{sec:datasets}). The entire camera network is modeled as a directed weighted spatial graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, W)$, where $\mathcal{V}$ represents the set of camera nodes ($N=608$ active camera stations across Ho Chi Minh City), $\mathcal{E}$ represents directed road connections, and $W$ is a Radial Basis Function (RBF) weighted distance matrix.

\subsubsection{Graph Edges and RBF Adjacency Matrix}
Graph edges $\mathcal{E}$ represent physical directed road connections between adjacent camera stations ($2,452$ valid directed edges). To reflect real-world urban road topology—where physical barriers (such as the Saigon River, expressways, and flyovers) or one-way traffic restrictions prevent unconstrained spatial spillover—directed edges $(i,j) \in \mathcal{E}$ are established strictly along valid physical traffic flow corridors derived from OpenStreetMap (OSM) network routing topology. Specifically, a directed edge is only created if there is a direct route from node $i$ to node $j$ without traversing any other intermediate camera node.

For any valid directed connection $(i,j) \in \mathcal{E}$, edge weight $W_{ij} \in [0, 1]$ is computed using a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel based on $d_{ij}$, which denotes the shortest-path network routing distance along valid physical road segments between node $i$ and node $j$ (in kilometers):
\begin{equation}
W_{ij} = \begin{cases} \exp\left(-\frac{d_{ij}^2}{\sigma^2}\right), & \text{if } (i,j) \in \mathcal{E} \text{ and } d_{ij} \le \tau \\ 0, & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma = 1.0887$ km is the empirical standard deviation of the valid directed road-network distances and $\tau = 3.3945$ km is the distance cutoff selected when constructing the sparse OSM graph. These values are descriptive graph-construction settings, not learned model parameters; we did not conduct a separate sensitivity study. If no physical road link connects node $i$ to node $j$, or if one-way traffic constraints prohibit direct movement from $i$ to $j$, $W_{ij} = 0$, ensuring that spatial graph convolutions propagate feature representations strictly along physically permissible urban flow paths.

\paragraph{Methodological Justification for Static Physical RBF Adjacency vs. Dynamic Graph Learning}
Recent spatio-temporal graph architectures (e.g., GraphWaveNet \cite{wavenet}, DSTAGNN \cite{dstagnn}) often employ dynamic or adaptive graph learning mechanisms, inferring spatial adjacency matrices directly from input node feature correlations via soft-attention or node embedding projections. A static graph cannot represent all functional relationships between distant locations, and this study does not establish its universal superiority over adaptive graphs. We retain a \textbf{Static OpenStreetMap (OSM) RBF Physical Adjacency Matrix} to test a constrained, interpretable alternative under camera-sensed noise for two practical reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Constraining Correlations under Perception Noise:} In two-stage camera-sensed ITS pipelines, Stage 2 inputs contain Stage 1 estimation errors (occlusion noise and transient camera dropouts). A physical road-distance cutoff ($\tau = 3.3945$ km) restricts message passing to plausible flow paths and reduces the opportunity for transient co-occurring visual errors to influence graph connectivity. This is a design rationale, not a proof that static graphs dominate adaptive graphs in general.
    \item \textbf{Memory and Computational Efficiency for Edge-Cloud Deployment:} Dynamic graph learning incurs quadratic memory complexity $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ to store and update dense $N \times N$ dynamic attention maps at every forward pass. For a city-scale network ($N=608$), this significantly inflates peak GPU activation memory (e.g., $11,509.1$ MB for STAEformer and $9,801.5$ MB for DSTAGNN). The static physical graph eliminates dynamic adjacency computation entirely, reducing peak activation memory to $8,606.9$ MB ($25.2\%$ lower than STAEformer) and enabling lightweight Edge-Cloud deployment without compromising structural spatial fidelity.
\end{enumerate}

To prepare the adjacency matrix for the Graph Convolutional Network (GCN), the weight matrix is symmetrically normalized. The normalized Graph Laplacian $L$ and Scaled Laplacian $\tilde{L}$ are computed as:
\begin{equation}
L = I_N - D^{-1/2} W D^{-1/2}, \quad \tilde{L} = \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}} L - I_N,
\end{equation}
where $D_{ii} = \sum_{j} W_{ij}$ is the diagonal degree matrix and $\lambda_{\max}$ is the maximum eigenvalue of $L$.

\subsubsection{Historical Feature Tensor Construction}
To model temporal congestion evolution across the network, the graph forecasting model receives a sliding window of historical node features. Let $\Delta t = 5$ minutes denote the temporal resolution of the graph sequence. While finer resolutions (e.g., 1-minute) can capture microscopic traffic dynamics, they introduce extreme temporal sparsity (zero-vehicle frames), amplify high-frequency sensor jitter caused by edge-device API dropouts, and drastically quadruple the sequence length, overwhelming GPU memory. A 5-minute aggregation provides the optimal macroscopic stabilization necessary to suppress perception noise while maintaining sufficient temporal granularity for predicting impending congestion. The input feature tensor $\mathcal{X}^{(t)}$ at time $t$ encompasses the previous $T_{\text{in}}$ observations:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{X}^{(t)} = \left[ X_{t-T_{\text{in}}+1}, X_{t-T_{\text{in}}+2}, \dots, X_t \right] \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times T_{\text{in}} \times F}
\end{equation}
where $N = 608$ is the number of nodes, $T_{\text{in}} = 24$ specifies a 120-minute historical receptive field, and $F = 5$ represents the input feature dimension (passenger car, motorcycle, and temporal encodings). 

In real-world camera deployments, transient API connection failures or camera downtimes occasionally result in missing visual payloads. To maintain the structural integrity of the temporal sequence, missing data points at any node $v_i$ are imputed using Linear Temporal Interpolation across the adjacent available time steps prior to Stage 2 tensor construction. Each node $v_i$ contains a feature vector $\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(t)} \in \mathbb{R}^F$ at time step $t$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Passenger car count $c_{\text{car}}^{(i,t)}$.
    \item Motorcycle count $c_{\text{bike}}^{(i,t)}$.
    \item Sine time-of-day feature: $\sin\left(\frac{2\pi \cdot \text{tod}}{1440}\right)$.
    \item Cosine time-of-day feature: $\cos\left(\frac{2\pi \cdot \text{tod}}{1440}\right)$.
    \item Normalized hour of day: $\frac{\text{hour}}{24}$.
\end{enumerate}

Given historical time series over $T_{\text{in}} = 24$ steps (120 minutes of history), the historical input tensor is $X \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times T_{\text{in}} \times N \times F}$ ($F=5$). The target is forecasting future category-specific traffic volumes across horizon $H = 6$ steps (30 minutes ahead): $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times H \times N \times 2}$.

TA-STGCN couples Chebyshev Spectral Graph Convolutions \cite{chebnet}, 1D Gated Temporal Convolutions (GLU) \cite{stgcn}, and Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention \cite{attention}. We are explicit that this is an architectural adaptation, not a new attention mechanism: self-attention in spatio-temporal forecasting is well established (ASTGCN \cite{astgcn}, GMAN \cite{gman}, STAEformer \cite{staeformer}), and we do not claim novelty for the attention operator itself. The specific design choice we isolate is its \emph{placement and scope}. Here, \emph{Model-Level} denotes that the attention module operates on the full temporal sequence of \emph{all} nodes simultaneously (a single global $T_{\text{in}}$-step temporal receptive field shared across the network), in contrast to (i) node-level temporal attention that would reweight each node's history independently, and (ii) the spatial-attention-first designs of ASTGCN/GMAN, which attend over nodes/edges and only secondarily over time. By inserting this global temporal reweighting \emph{between} the two ST-Conv blocks---after spatial aggregation, before final temporal projection---TA-STGCN decouples spatial Chebyshev convolutions from a single shared temporal-context step, rather than stacking per-node or per-edge attention. We treat this as a design point to be benchmarked, not as a conceptual advance over the cited attention models. Figure~\ref{fig:ta_stgcn_architecture} illustrates the structural diagram of the proposed TA-STGCN architecture.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig/ta_stgcn_architecture.pdf}
\caption{Architectural overview of the proposed TA-STGCN model. (a) The overall forecasting pipeline processes $F=5$ input features through 2 ST-Conv blocks with a Temporal Self-Attention module inserted in the middle ($C=80$, Depth=2). (b) The Temporal Self-Attention block establishes a global receptive field via multi-head attention ($h=4$) and a feed-forward network to filter high-frequency perception noise before final output projection.}

TA-STGCN extends the standard Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (STGCN) \cite{stgcn}. The core architecture extracts spatio-temporal features using 2 ST-Conv blocks ($\text{Depth}=2$) composed of 1D Gated Temporal Convolutions (GLU) and Chebyshev Spectral Graph Convolutions ($K=3$) \cite{chebnet} with $C=80$ hidden channels. While 1D convolutions efficiently capture localized transitions, their finite receptive fields struggle under motorcycle-dominant mixed traffic. Dense motorcycle streams generate high-frequency count fluctuations and temporary camera occlusions, which rigid 1D convolutions propagate as estimation noise into multi-step forecasts.

To address this, TA-STGCN inserts a \textbf{Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention} module \cite{attention} ($h=4$ attention heads) in the middle position (between the first and second ST-Conv blocks) globally across the temporal sequence dimension ($T_{\text{in}} = 24$). By computing pairwise temporal affinity weights $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ via the Softmax operation across all historical steps, the self-attention mechanism establishes a global receptive field. To build intuition, the following stylized calculation averages independent perturbations under fixed weights; its relationship to the learned, data-dependent module is assessed empirically rather than asserted:

\textbf{Conditional Variance Intuition:}
Let $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{s} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_{\text{in}}}$ be an observed traffic time-series vector. If, conditionally on fixed weights $\boldsymbol{\alpha} \in \Delta^{T_{\text{in}}-1}$, the perturbations $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ are independent, zero-mean, and have common variance $\sigma_{\epsilon}^2$, then:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:attn_var}
\text{Var}\left( \sum_{t=1}^{T_{\text{in}}} \alpha_t \epsilon_t \right) = \sigma_{\epsilon}^2 \sum_{t=1}^{T_{\text{in}}} \alpha_t^2 = \frac{\sigma_{\epsilon}^2}{T_{\text{eff}}},
\end{equation}
where $T_{\text{eff}} = 1 / \|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_2^2 \in [1, T_{\text{in}}]$ is the effective attention window. We present this identity deliberately as a \emph{stylized intuition}, not a guarantee: in TA-STGCN the weights are data-dependent and the observed perception errors are asymmetric and heteroscedastic, so the identity does not by itself prove variance reduction. Its role is to motivate \emph{why} a learned global temporal reweighting has the \emph{capacity} to temper independent perturbations; whether the trained module actually exhibits this behavior, and against which perturbation types, is settled empirically by the placement ablation, attention-weight analysis, and noise simulations in Section~\ref{sec:results}. Uniform average pooling would minimize the conditional variance in this stylized setting, but can blur meaningful traffic shifts. The learned module instead has the capacity to trade temporal smoothing against responsiveness, and multiple heads provide several learned temporal representations rather than a formal variance optimum.

Real perception noise in mixed traffic is \emph{not} zero-mean: as established in Section~\ref{sec:results}, Stage 1 backbones exhibit systematic undercounting under heavy occlusion. The empirical simulations therefore test performance under asymmetric, heteroscedastic perturbations directly, supporting a comparative robustness observation specific to the evaluated data and noise settings.

The attention output tensor is then passed through a 1D temporal convolution to collapse the historical time dimension into the target forecast horizon $H=6$, producing predictions $\hat{Y}$. The network is optimized end-to-end using the Huber Loss \cite{huber}.

Algorithm~\ref{algo:ta_stgcn_forward} details the exact forward pass operations and tensor shape transformations of the TA-STGCN architecture.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{TA-STGCN Forward Pass (Depth=2, Middle Temporal Attention)}
\label{algo:ta_stgcn_forward}
\DontPrintSemicolon
\KwIn{Historical traffic tensor $\mathcal{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times T_{\text{in}} \times N \times F}$, Scaled Graph Laplacian $\tilde{L} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$.}
\KwOut{Multi-horizon category-specific forecast $\hat{\mathcal{Y}} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times H \times N \times 2}$}
\tcp{Initial Feature Permutation and Feature Scaling}
$\mathcal{H} \leftarrow \text{Permute}(\mathcal{X}) \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times F \times N \times T_{\text{in}}}$\;
\tcp{First ST-Conv Block ($C=80$ hidden channels)}
$\mathcal{H}_{\text{tmp1}} \leftarrow \text{GLU\_TemporalConv}_{1 \times K_t}(\mathcal{H})$ \tcp*{$\mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times N \times T_{\text{in}}}$}
$\mathcal{H}_{\text{sp1}} \leftarrow \text{ChebConv}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{tmp1}}, \tilde{L})$ \tcp*{$K=3$ Chebyshev order}
$\mathcal{H}_1 \leftarrow \text{GLU\_TemporalConv}_{1 \times K_t}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{sp1}}) + \mathcal{H}_{\text{res1}}$ \tcp*{$\mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times N \times T_{\text{in}}}$}

\tcp{Temporal Self-Attention Module ($h=4$ heads, Middle Position)}
Reshape $\mathcal{H}_1$ to $\mathcal{H}_{\text{seq}} \in \mathbb{R}^{(B \times N) \times T_{\text{in}} \times C}$\;
$\mathbf{Q} \leftarrow \mathcal{H}_{\text{seq}}\mathbf{W}_Q, \quad \mathbf{K} \leftarrow \mathcal{H}_{\text{seq}}\mathbf{W}_K, \quad \mathbf{V} \leftarrow \mathcal{H}_{\text{seq}}\mathbf{W}_V$\;
$\boldsymbol{\alpha} \leftarrow \text{Softmax}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)$ \tcp*{Pairwise temporal affinity}
$\mathcal{H}_{\text{attn}} \leftarrow \text{MultiHeadAttn}(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}) + \mathcal{H}_{\text{seq}}$ \tcp*{Residual connection}
Reshape $\mathcal{H}_{\text{attn}}$ back to $\mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times N \times T_{\text{in}}}$\;

\tcp{Second ST-Conv Block ($C=80$ hidden channels)}
$\mathcal{H}_{\text{tmp2}} \leftarrow \text{GLU\_TemporalConv}_{1 \times K_t}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{attn}})$ \tcp*{$\mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times N \times T_{\text{in}}}$}
$\mathcal{H}_{\text{sp2}} \leftarrow \text{ChebConv}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{tmp2}}, \tilde{L})$ \tcp*{$K=3$ Chebyshev order}
$\mathcal{H}_2 \leftarrow \text{GLU\_TemporalConv}_{1 \times K_t}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{sp2}}) + \mathcal{H}_{\text{res2}}$ \tcp*{$\mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times N \times T_{\text{in}}}$}

\tcp{Output Projection}
Reshape $\mathcal{H}_2$ to $\mathcal{H}_{\text{out}} \in \mathbb{R}^{(B \times N) \times C \times T_{\text{in}}}$\;
$\hat{\mathcal{Y}}_{\text{flat}} \leftarrow \text{Conv1D}_{K_t=T_{\text{in}}}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{out}})$ \tcp*{Project to $(H \times 2)$ channels}
Reshape $\hat{\mathcal{Y}}_{\text{flat}}$ to $\hat{\mathcal{Y}} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times H \times N \times 2}$ \tcp*{$H=6$ horizons, 2 categories}
\Return $\hat{\mathcal{Y}}$\;
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Camera Network Acquisition}
We harvest surveillance camera feeds and topological metadata from the public Ho Chi Minh City Traffic Portal via open civic access provided for public transportation monitoring and non-commercial scientific research. To establish the spatial graph nodes, we deploy an automated pipeline using standard client-side session management to parse public camera locations and extract geographical coordinates (Latitude, Longitude) for each camera station. All visual feeds are publicly accessible snapshots, processed strictly for non-commercial academic research without recording any personally identifiable information. 

For continuous visual perception, an automated multi-threaded API downloader with HTTP session pooling is deployed to query the image endpoints. To ensure strict temporal synchronization across the network, the downloader operates on a rigid cyclical schedule, scanning all initial 657 cameras exactly every 60 seconds. The acquired raw images are automatically organized into a time-aware hierarchical directory structure to facilitate efficient batch processing. After rigorously filtering out transient API connection failures, "Image not available" error payloads, and consistently offline feeds, \textbf{608 active camera stations} are established as the final graph node set $\mathcal{V}$ ($N = |\mathcal{V}| = 608$).

An automated multi-threaded API downloader with HTTP session pooling scans all 608 cameras every 60 seconds, building a synchronized, time-aligned sequence of traffic images. Figure~\ref{fig:api_samples} shows representative traffic camera feeds collected from the Ho Chi Minh City Traffic Portal API across major urban intersections. \begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Multi-Threaded Camera API Harvesting and Data Imputation}
\label{algo:api_harvesting}
\DontPrintSemicolon
\KwIn{Set of camera URLs $\mathcal{U} = \{u_1, \dots, u_N\}$ ($N=608$), Fetch interval $\Delta t = 1$ min, Max threads $M=32$.}
\KwOut{Synchronized vehicle count dataset $\mathcal{D}$}
Initialize HTTP session pool with $M$ concurrent thread workers\;
$\mathcal{D} \leftarrow \emptyset$\;
\While{data collection is active}{
    $t \leftarrow \text{GetCurrentTime}()$\;
    $\mathbf{c}^{(t)} \leftarrow \text{Array of NaNs with size } N \times 2$\;
    \tcp{Parallel Fetching (Stage 1)}
    \ForEach{$u_i \in \mathcal{U}$ \textbf{in parallel}}{
        $\text{img} \leftarrow \text{HTTP\_GET}(u_i, \text{timeout}=5s)$\;
        \If{\text{img} is valid and not corrupted}{
            $[\hat{c}_{\text{car}}, \hat{c}_{\text{bike}}]^T \leftarrow \text{Stage1\_Predict}(\text{img})$\;
            $\mathbf{c}^{(t)}_i \leftarrow [\hat{c}_{\text{car}}, \hat{c}_{\text{bike}}]^T$\;
        }
    }
    $\mathcal{D} \leftarrow \mathcal{D} \cup \{\mathbf{c}^{(t)}\}$\;

}
\tcp{Offline Temporal Imputation (Double Rolling)}
\ForEach{$i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$}{
    Find missing timestamps $t$ where $\mathbf{c}^{(t)}_i$ is NaN\;
    \ForEach{missing $t$}{
        $\mathbf{c}^{(t)}_i \leftarrow \text{LinearInterpolate}(\mathbf{c}^{(t-\dots)}_i, \mathbf{c}^{(t+\dots)}_i)$\;
    }
    Aggregate $\mathbf{c}^{(t)}_i$ to 5-minute intervals ($\Delta t_{\text{new}} = 5$ min)\;
}
\Return $\mathcal{D}$\;
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Stage 1: Vision Dataset}
The camera vehicle estimation dataset consists of \textbf{5,012 manually annotated traffic images} captured comprehensively from an initial pool of \textbf{657 surveillance cameras} across the Ho Chi Minh City Traffic Portal. The data collection spanned from June 23, 2025, to September 16, 2025, capturing diverse lighting and traffic conditions throughout the day. Critically, these images are drawn from the same camera network that forms the Stage 2 graph, so every one of the 608 active Stage 2 nodes is represented by annotated imagery in Stage 1; the pseudo-labels propagated to Stage 2 therefore originate from cameras whose visual appearance the Stage 1 backbones have seen during training or evaluation. To prevent the vision model from memorizing static camera background geometry, camera locations in the Validation (501 images) and Testing (501 images) sets are partitioned across disjoint intersections from those in the Training set (4,010 images), evaluating visual generalization under unseen camera perspectives.

\textbf{Vehicle Density Distribution.} Statistical analysis of the annotations reveals an average of 30.28 vehicles per image ($\sigma = 25.07$), with a median of 24.00. The data exhibits a distinct right-skewed distribution, ranging from completely empty streets (0 vehicles) to instances of extreme congestion (up to 194 vehicles in a single frame). Categorically, motorcycles heavily dominate the traffic flow (mean: 23.02, max: 178) compared to passenger cars (mean: 7.26, max: 50). This distribution underscores the prevalence of moderate traffic alongside critical, hyper-dense edge cases.

\textbf{Addressing Dataset Limitations and Generalization.} We state this limitation directly rather than hedging it: 4,010 training images is a \emph{small} sample for representing the visual diversity of 608 distinct camera perspectives, backgrounds, and lighting conditions, and it is the binding constraint of this study's Stage 1. Three concrete consequences follow. First, for a continuous counting regression task, heavy feature extractors such as the $\sim$86M-parameter ViT-Base backbone are data-hungry relative to this sample; this is a primary reason the Transformer backbones underperform and diverge across seeds (Table~\ref{tab:stage1_seed_variance}), and we do not interpret that underperformance as a general verdict on Transformers. Second, Stage 2 is trained on pseudo-labels produced by Stage 1, so the 5{,}012-image ceiling caps the upper bound of the entire two-stage pipeline's forecasting fidelity---improving Stage 1 generalization is the most direct lever for improving Stage 2. Third, because the validation/test camera locations are disjoint from training (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), the reported Stage 1 errors are measured on \emph{unseen} perspectives and are therefore a generalization estimate, but one computed on a 501-image test set, which limits its statistical power for per-camera or per-density subpopulation claims.

To mitigate this limitation and expand visual diversity without incurring additional manual annotation costs, specialized traffic-scene data augmentations are essential. These strategies include targeted random cropping of dense vehicle regions—forcing the model to learn localized vehicle features rather than relying on global spatial priors—and simulating artificial congestion to oversample the long-tail distribution of high-density scenarios. We treat these augmentations as partial mitigations, not a substitute for more data: the principled remedies are larger-scale annotation, semi-/self-supervised pre-training on the unlabeled camera streams the pipeline already harvests, and matched point-supervision that would enable comparison with specialized dense counters. These are explicitly listed as future work in Section~\ref{sec:deployment_limitations}.

To verify ground-truth labeling consistency across these dense vehicle clusters while managing manual annotation costs, a statistically significant random subset of 500 images (approximately 10\% of the dataset) was selected for cross-verification. This subset was independently annotated by three trained human annotators to establish baseline consistency before distributing the remaining workload. Inter-annotator reliability was quantified using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient ($\text{ICC}(3,k)$ two-way mixed-effects model for absolute agreement). The evaluation yielded an $\text{ICC} = 0.984$ for passenger car counts and $\text{ICC} = 0.962$ for motorcycle counts (overall combined $\text{ICC} = 0.973, p < 0.01$), confirming high general annotation consistency ($\text{ICC} > 0.90$). 

However, stratifying the analysis by traffic density reveals distinct challenges in edge cases. Under low-density conditions, annotator agreement remains near-perfect ($\text{ICC} > 0.99$). Conversely, under extreme congestion and severe partial occlusions (e.g., dense clusters of motorcycles weaving at signalized intersections), the motorcycle ICC drops to $0.941$, reflecting the inherent visual ambiguity human annotators face in highly unstructured mixed traffic. Following annotation, all images are resized to $224 \times 224$ pixels and normalized with ImageNet mean and standard deviation.

\paragraph{Stage 2: Traffic Graph Time-Series Dataset}
The data for Stage 2 is derived from the vision predictions generated in Stage 1. Initially, data was collected from a pool of 657 surveillance cameras. However, to ensure the integrity of the time-series forecasting, 49 cameras were excluded due to excessive missing timestamps that could not be reliably interpolated. Consequently, the remaining 608 active camera nodes form the spatial basis of the forecasting graph. The Stage 2 time-series spans the same collection window as Stage 1 (June 23, 2025, to September 16, 2025, approximately 86 days), during which the multi-threaded API pipeline continuously harvested synchronized 1-minute camera snapshots across the network. After aggregation to 5-minute intervals, the resulting chronological sequence per node is partitioned into the Train (80\%), Validation (10\%), and Test (10\%) splits described below.

The raw prediction outputs are first processed at a 1-minute temporal resolution. Due to occasional camera latency and system interruptions, there are missing timestamps within the data streams. To reconstruct a continuous time-series, a complete chronological timeline at a 1-minute frequency is generated for each camera node, spanning from its earliest to its latest recording. Missing timestamps are identified and filled using time-based linear interpolation (\textit{method="time"}), ensuring that brief gaps are smoothly bridged based on adjacent traffic states. Any remaining missing values (e.g., at the very edges of the timeline) are handled via backward and forward filling, and the resulting continuous counts are rounded to the nearest integer.

Following this 1-minute level reconstruction, the time series are aggregated into 5-minute intervals ($step\_minutes=5$) to form the final input sequences. These aggregated sequences are further smoothed via a double-rolling transformation ($window_1=3$, $window_2=5$) to mitigate high-frequency noise inherent in raw traffic counts.

\textbf{Per-Node Z-Score Normalization.} To ensure stable gradient propagation and eliminate scale bias across heterogeneous camera locations (where high-traffic intersections may observe $>100$ vehicles while residential streets observe $<5$), all vehicle count time-series are standardized using per-node Z-score normalization:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{v}_{i,t} = \frac{v_{i,t} - \mu_i}{\sigma_i + \epsilon}, \quad \epsilon = 10^{-6},
\end{equation}
where $\mu_i$ and $\sigma_i$ denote the per-node temporal mean and standard deviation computed \emph{strictly on the Training split}. 

\textbf{Strict Chronological Splitting.} To rigorously evaluate forecasting capability on future unseen data, the time-series dataset is partitioned along the temporal axis into Train (80\%), Validation (10\%), and Test (10\%) splits. This partition strictly preserves chronological order without any randomized temporal shuffling. The testing phase operates exclusively on the final 10\% contiguous window of the timeline, guaranteeing that all forecasted ``future'' time steps ($t+1$ to $t+6$) occur chronologically after the training and validation horizons. Furthermore, all $\mu_i, \sigma_i$ normalization statistics and double-rolling moving averages are computed solely on the historical Training window and applied forward to the Validation and Test windows, strictly preventing any backward temporal lookahead or data leakage.

Historical lookback length is configured as $T_{\text{in}} = 24$ steps (120 minutes of history), and the target forecast horizon is $H = 6$ steps (30 minutes ahead).

\paragraph{Strict Two-Stage Disjoint Isolation and Zero-Leakage Protocol}
To guarantee strict experimental isolation and eliminate camera-level data leakage between the Stage 1 vision perception backbone and Stage 2 graph forecasting models:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Disjoint Camera Splits for Stage 1 Perception Training:} The Stage 1 vision models (ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B5, ViT-Base/16, ConvNeXt-Tiny, and MobileNet-V3) are trained strictly on traffic images harvested from the Stage 1 Training camera locations (4,010 images). Camera stations assigned to the Stage 1 Validation (501 images) and Testing (501 images) sets are strictly partitioned across disjoint intersections from those in the Training set, evaluating visual count regression under unseen camera perspectives.
    \item \textbf{Strict Stage 2 Graph Training Isolation:} Stage 2 graph forecasting models are trained on vehicle count time-series streams generated by Stage 1 over the Stage 2 Training split (first 80\% contiguous window). Stage 2 loss optimization strictly utilizes Stage 1 predictions from training camera locations over the training window.
    \item \textbf{Zero Temporal and Spatial Leakage Guarantee:} All final Stage 2 multi-horizon forecasting benchmarks on the Test set (final 10\% contiguous window) are evaluated on vehicle count streams generated by Stage 1 on unseen camera views. No test-set camera images or future time steps are ever accessible during Stage 1 model training or Stage 2 parameter optimization, guaranteeing zero spatial or temporal data leakage. Because all 608 Stage 2 nodes are covered by Stage 1 imagery, the pseudo-labels used for graph forecasting are not extrapolated to cameras whose appearance is entirely unknown to the perception backbones.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Baseline Architectures and Configuration}
\label{sec:baselines_config}

To establish a baseline comparison for Stage 2, we evaluate baseline architectures against our proposed TA-STGCN model \cite{stgcn, attention}. Baseline models are configured with parameter capacities to evaluate whether TA-STGCN's performance advantage arises from architectural innovation rather than parameter scaling.

\subsubsection{Evaluated Baseline Architectures}
To establish a comprehensive benchmark for Stage 2, we evaluate six representative baseline architectures spanning spatial-temporal convolutional networks, attention-based graph networks, and recent SOTA Transformer models:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Baseline 1 (Standard STGCN):} The foundational convolutional STGNN introduced by Yu et al. \cite{stgcn} (IJCAI 2018), utilizing 1D temporal Gated Linear Units (GLU) and Chebyshev spectral graph convolutions ($K=3$) \cite{chebnet} without attention mechanisms.
    \item \textbf{Baseline 2 (GraphWaveNet):} Wu et al. \cite{wavenet} (IJCAI 2019), combining adaptive graph adjacency matrix learning with stacked 1D dilated causal temporal convolutions to capture dynamic spatial-temporal dependencies.
    \item \textbf{Baseline 3 (ASTGCN):} Guo et al. \cite{astgcn} (AAAI 2019), incorporating spatial and temporal attention mechanisms within Chebyshev graph convolution blocks to dynamically capture dynamic spatio-temporal correlations.
    \item \textbf{Baseline 4 (DSTAGNN):} Lan et al. \cite{dstagnn} (ICML 2022), a Dynamic Spatial-Temporal Aware Graph Neural Network introducing dynamic graph construction and spatial-temporal attention.
    \item \textbf{Baseline 5 (MegaCRN):} Jiang et al. \cite{megacrn} (AAAI 2023), a Meta-Graph Convolutional Recurrent Network capturing spatio-temporal dynamics via meta-learning node embeddings.
    \item \textbf{Baseline 6 (STAEformer):} Liu et al. \cite{staeformer} (CIKM 2023), applying Spatio-Temporal Adaptive Embeddings to Transformer encoder blocks.
    \item \textbf{Baseline 7 (iTransformer):} Liu et al. \cite{itransformer} (ICLR 2024), an Inverted Transformer architecture applying self-attention over individual variates/nodes and feed-forward networks across temporal steps.
    \item \textbf{TA-STGCN (Proposed / Ours):} Our proposed architecture coupling lightweight ST-Conv blocks \cite{stgcn} with Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention ($h=4$) \cite{attention} as described in Section~\ref{sec:graph_tastgcn}.
\end{enumerate}

The architectural choices in Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison} highlight three key computational trade-offs:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Spatial Convolution Operators vs. Dynamic Embeddings:} Standard STGCN \cite{stgcn} and TA-STGCN utilize 3rd-order Chebyshev Spectral Polynomials ($K=3$) \cite{chebnet} over a physical road distance graph, capturing localized multi-hop spatial dependencies with linear spatial complexity. In contrast, recent SOTA models (DSTAGNN \cite{dstagnn}, MegaCRN \cite{megacrn}, STAEformer \cite{staeformer}) rely on dense dynamic graph soft-attention or high-dimensional embeddings, incurring high memory allocation during training on city-scale 608-node networks.
    \item \textbf{Temporal Sequence Modeling Mechanisms:} While 1D Gated Temporal Convolutions (GLU) \cite{stgcn} possess fixed local receptive fields ($K_t=3$), SOTA Transformer baselines (STAEformer \cite{staeformer}, DSTAGNN \cite{dstagnn}) apply global attention over historical sequences. TA-STGCN bridges this gap by augmenting lightweight ST-Conv blocks with a Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention module ($h=4$) \cite{attention}, dynamically weighing temporal correlations across the entire 120-minute history without quadratic spatial memory explosion.
    \item \textbf{Controlled Capacity Design:} TA-STGCN maintains a balanced parameter footprint ($453,052$ parameters). While it introduces a marginal 20.7\% parameter increase over the lightweight STGCN Baseline ($375,212$ parameters) due to the multi-head attention projection layers, it achieves a substantial 70.4\% parameter reduction compared to heavy baselines like ASTGCN ($1,532,566$ parameters) while offering superior noise robustness and training stability.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Training Hyperparameters}
All models across both sub-problems are implemented in PyTorch 2.x and trained hardware-accelerated on \textbf{two NVIDIA T4 GPUs (16 GB VRAM each)}.

The vision backbones (ResNet-50 \cite{resnet}, EfficientNet-B5 \cite{efficientnet}, ViT-Base/16 \cite{vit}, ConvNeXt-Tiny \cite{convnext}, and MobileNet-V3 \cite{mobilenet}) with unified MLP regression heads are trained under the following setup:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stage 1 Optimizer:} AdamW optimizer with initial learning rate $\eta = 3 \times 10^{-4}$ and weight decay coefficient $1 \times 10^{-2}$.
    \item \textbf{Learning Rate Schedule:} Cosine Annealing Learning Rate Scheduler decaying down to $\eta_{\min} = 1 \times 10^{-6}$.
    \item \textbf{Epochs and Batch Size:} Models are trained for 120 epochs with a mini-batch size of $B = 32$ and an Early Stopping patience of 20 epochs.
    \item \textbf{Loss Function:} Smooth $L_1$ (Huber) Loss \cite{huber} with threshold $\beta = 1.0$.
    \item \textbf{Data Augmentations:} Random horizontal flips (probability $p=0.5$), color jittering ($\pm 15\%$ brightness, contrast, and saturation), and random spatial cropping resized to $224 \times 224$.
\end{itemize}

All spatio-temporal graph forecasting models (STGCN \cite{stgcn}, GraphWaveNet \cite{wavenet}, ASTGCN \cite{astgcn}, STAEformer \cite{staeformer}, MegaCRN \cite{megacrn}, DSTAGNN \cite{dstagnn}, iTransformer \cite{itransformer}, and TA-STGCN) as well as all Ablation Study variants are trained under the following standardized setup:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Standardized Benchmarking Protocol:} All baseline models (STGCN, GraphWaveNet, ASTGCN, STAEformer, MegaCRN, DSTAGNN, and iTransformer) use the same optimizer, training budget, Huber objective, and early-stopping rule. Their internal architectures follow the authors' recommended configurations adapted to the shared input/output dimensions. We did not perform an exhaustive, model-specific hyperparameter search on the 608-node benchmark; results should consequently be interpreted as a controlled implementation comparison, with possible sensitivity of individual baselines to further tuning acknowledged as a limitation.
    \item \textbf{Hardware Infrastructure:} All graph experiments across benchmark baselines and ablation variants are executed hardware-accelerated on \textbf{two NVIDIA T4 GPUs (16~GB VRAM each)} under PyTorch 2.x.
    \item \textbf{Stage 2 Optimizer:} Adam optimizer \cite{adam} with initial learning rate $\eta = 1 \times 10^{-3}$ and weight decay coefficient $1 \times 10^{-4}$.
    \item \textbf{Learning Rate Schedule:} \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} scheduler decaying the learning rate by a factor of $\gamma = 0.5$ if validation loss fails to decrease for 10 consecutive epochs ($\text{patience} = 10$).
    \item \textbf{Epochs and Early Stopping:} Trained for up to $N_{\text{max}} = 120$ epochs with an Early Stopping threshold of 20 epochs ($\text{patience} = 20$) monitoring validation loss to prevent overfitting and guarantee fair convergence across variants.
    \item \textbf{Batch Size:} Training mini-batch size $B = 64$ (evaluation mini-batch size $B = 64$).
    \item \textbf{Loss Function:} Huber Loss \cite{huber} ($\delta = 1.0$). The Huber loss acts as $L_2$ (MSE) for small errors and $L_1$ (MAE) for large errors. We apply this common objective to every model to isolate architectural differences under the same outlier treatment. This choice may not match every baseline's original training loss and therefore trades model-specific optimal tuning for experimental control.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Seed Evaluation Protocol:} Evaluated across independent random seeds. All reported evaluation metrics present $\text{Mean} \pm \text{Std}$ across random seeds.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Evaluation Metrics}
% NOTE (Round 1): the separate MSE equation was removed as redundant
% (RMSE = sqrt(MSE)); the MSE row in the benchmark table was removed accordingly.
Model performance is evaluated using Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE):
\begin{equation}
\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{N \cdot H \cdot M} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{h=1}^{H} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \left| y_{i,h}^{(k)} - \hat{y}_{i,h}^{(k)} \right|,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{N \cdot H \cdot M} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{h=1}^{H} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \left( y_{i,h}^{(k)} - \hat{y}_{i,h}^{(k)} \right)^2 },
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{MAPE} = \frac{1}{|\Omega|} \sum_{(i,h,k) \in \Omega} \frac{\left| y_{i,h}^{(k)} - \hat{y}_{i,h}^{(k)} \right|}{y_{i,h}^{(k)} + \epsilon} \times 100\%,
\end{equation}
where $N=608$ graph nodes, $H=6$ forecast steps, $M$ is the test batch count, $\Omega = \{ (i,h,k) \mid y_{i,h}^{(k)} > 0.5 \}$ excludes near-zero traffic intervals, and $\epsilon = 10^{-5}$ is solely a numerical safeguard. Since each retained target exceeds $0.5$, $\epsilon$ is at least five orders of magnitude smaller than the denominator and has negligible effect on the reported MAPE; the exclusion prevents percentage errors at near-zero night-time counts from dominating the metric.

\paragraph{Reporting Units (De-Normalization)}
All MAE, RMSE, and MAPE values reported in this paper---for both Stage 1 (per-image counting) and Stage 2 (graph forecasting)---are computed on \emph{de-normalized vehicle counts}, i.e., in units of whole vehicles, not in the Z-score-normalized space used internally for optimization. For Stage 1, the regression head predicts counts directly in vehicles and metrics are computed on raw counts. For Stage 2, the per-node Z-score normalization of Section~\ref{sec:datasets} is applied only to the model's inputs and targets during training; at evaluation, predictions and ground truth are inverse-transformed back to real vehicle counts via $y_{\text{real}} = y_{\text{z}} \cdot \sigma_i + \mu_i$ (with $\mu_i, \sigma_i$ computed strictly on the Training split) before MAE/RMSE/MAPE are computed. Consequently every MAE in Tables~\ref{tab:stage1_results}, \ref{tab:benchmark_5seeds}, \ref{tab:stage2_density_decomposed}, and \ref{tab:noise_robustness} is an absolute error in vehicles per 5-minute step per node (Stage 2) or vehicles per image (Stage 1), and the density-regime thresholds in Table~\ref{tab:stage2_density_decomposed} (Low $<10$, Medium $10$--$25$, High $>25$) are defined on these same real counts, matching the Stage 1 thresholds in Table~\ref{tab:stage1_density}. We note one caveat for interpretation: the Stage 2 overall MAE is an unweighted mean over all $608$ nodes, all six horizons, and both categories, including low-density residential nodes and off-peak windows whose near-zero counts contribute small absolute errors and thus pull the overall MAE downward; the density-stratified table (Table~\ref{tab:stage2_density_decomposed}) is the more informative view of error magnitude at operationally relevant congestion levels.

\paragraph{Statistical vs. Practical Significance Analysis}
When evaluating model performance across massive spatio-temporal datasets ($N=608$ nodes, $H=6$ horizons, $M \approx 5,000$ test sequence windows, totaling over $1.8 \times 10^7$ prediction points), standard hypothesis tests almost universally yield extreme statistical significance ($p < 0.001$). As noted in Section~\ref{sec:variance_analysis}, with this sample size even trivially small differences become statistically significant, so we emphasize \emph{effect-size} (Cohen's $d$) rather than $p$-values throughout.

On unperturbed, clean traffic feeds, the overall point-forecasting improvement of TA-STGCN ($\text{MAE} = 3.2401 \pm 0.0108, \text{MAPE} = 11.32\% \pm 0.04\%$) over GraphWaveNet ($\text{MAE} = 3.2776 \pm 0.0049$) and the STGCN Baseline ($\text{MAE} = 3.2703 \pm 0.0128$) is small in absolute terms ($\approx 0.03$--$0.04$ MAE) with a negligible Cohen's $d$ effect size ($d = -0.024$ and $d = -0.032$, respectively). We therefore do \emph{not} claim a clean-input point-accuracy victory: under ideal, noise-free operational conditions, the leading spatio-temporal graph models are practically equivalent in point-forecasting accuracy. Scaling the STGCN baseline to a larger capacity (STGCN Large, 487K parameters, exceeding TA-STGCN's 453K) does \emph{not} improve accuracy ($\text{MAE} = 3.2805 \pm 0.0134$), confirming that TA-STGCN's residual advantage stems from its temporal-attention inductive bias rather than parameter count.

The practical relevance of TA-STGCN in this benchmark therefore does not rest on the marginal clean-input gain, but on a different and, we argue, deployment-relevant axis. \textbf{TA-STGCN is best read as a robustness-oriented adaptation of its STGCN predecessor:} inserting the temporal self-attention module costs a negligible clean-input effect ($d = -0.032$, practically equivalent) yet converts into a \emph{large} effect under perception noise. This robustness-vs-cost asymmetry is the core contribution, and it manifests across three operational dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Noise Resilience Relative to the STGCN Predecessor (Primary Claim):} The same module that is practically equivalent on clean inputs ($d = -0.032$ vs.\ STGCN Baseline) yields a \emph{large} effect under simulated perception noise. At Level 2 ($\Delta\text{MAE}=4.64$, the mean empirical Stage 1 error), TA-STGCN improves over STGCN Baseline with Cohen's $d = +2.004$; at Level 4 ($\Delta \text{MAE} = 13.95$, Table~\ref{tab:noise_robustness}), TA-STGCN obtains $10.1532 \pm 1.1153$ MAE versus $13.1724 \pm 0.3515$ for the STGCN Baseline, $d = +3.651$. In other words, the attention insertion trades an essentially free clean-input cost for a structurally large robustness gain over the architecture it adapts---the qualitative behavior the design was intended to produce. The effect \emph{stratifies by competitor class} (Table~\ref{tab:noise_robustness_effects}): the decisive and consistent separation is against the \emph{non-attention} graph/RNN baselines (GraphWaveNet $d = +5.84$, MegaCRN $d = +4.95$, DSTAGNN $d = +3.25$, ASTGCN $d = +3.48$, STGCN $d = +3.65$ at Level 4), and it widens with noise severity. Against the \emph{attention-based} competitors the margin is only small-to-medium and not a decisive separation: STAEformer $d = 0.279$ at Level 2 (small) and $0.592$ at Level 4 (medium); iTransformer $d = 0.669$ at Level 4, comparable to the run-to-run spread. The robustness contribution is therefore best stated at the design-class level---attention-based temporal modeling is substantially more robust to perception noise than non-attention graph/RNN modeling on this benchmark---rather than as TA-STGCN dominating the SOTA attention baselines, with which it is on the same robustness order while being more parameter-economical.
    \item \textbf{Flatter Long-Horizon Error Accumulation:} At extended 30-minute lookaheads ($t+6$), TA-STGCN bounds error propagation more effectively than GraphWaveNet ($t+6$ MAE $= 3.4947$ vs.\ $3.5800$, a reduction of $0.0853$ MAE and $0.21\%$ MAPE). While this absolute gap is small, it widens monotonically with horizon and compounds across all $608$ nodes and every forecast cycle, which is the regime in which traffic-management decisions (signal-timing and incident-response lead times) are most sensitive to error drift.
    \item \textbf{Hardware Activation Memory Economy:} TA-STGCN reduces peak GPU activation memory by \textbf{25.2\%} compared to STAEformer ($8,606.9$ MB vs.\ $11,509.1$ MB) and trainable parameter count by \textbf{70.4\%} compared to ASTGCN ($453\text{K}$ vs.\ $1,533\text{K}$). It is not the most memory-economical model overall---iTransformer's $265.2$ MB reflects its non-graph inverted design and is treated separately (Section~\ref{sec:deployment_limitations})---but among graph-structured baselines that materialize an $N\times N$ spatial map, TA-STGCN occupies a favorable accuracy--memory point for cost-constrained municipal deployment.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Impact of Temporal Attention} Removing the Temporal Attention module degrades overall performance from $3.2401 \pm 0.0108$ ($\text{MAPE} = 11.32\%$) for TA-STGCN to $3.2703 \pm 0.0128$ ($\text{MAPE} = 11.44\%$) for STGCN Baseline, reiterating that global temporal attention mitigates error compounding over extended lookaheads.

\paragraph{Model Capacity vs. Architectural Induction (Fair Parameter Comparison)} To examine whether the observed difference may reflect temporal attention rather than parameter expansion, we evaluate \textbf{STGCN (Large)} ($C=96$ hidden channels), which has \textbf{487K parameters}, exceeding TA-STGCN's 453K. In Table~\ref{tab:ablation_study}, this larger STGCN has MAE $3.2805 \pm 0.0134$ ($\text{MAPE} = 11.53\% \pm 0.11\%$). This controlled comparison is consistent with a benefit from the inserted attention module, although it cannot isolate every architecture--optimization interaction. Inserting attention increases peak GPU activation memory from $4,837.6$ MB to $8,606.9$ MB because of the $T_{\text{in}} \times T_{\text{in}}$ attention map; the resulting accuracy, robustness, and memory trade-off should be assessed against deployment constraints.

\paragraph{Placement of the Temporal Attention Module} We further examine where the attention module should be inserted within the ST-Conv stack. Placing attention \emph{before} the first ST-Conv block (Attention at Before) yields $\text{MAE} = 3.2485 \pm 0.0087$, while placing it \emph{after} the second block (Attention at End) degrades performance further to $\text{MAE} = 3.2680 \pm 0.0099$. The canonical \emph{middle} placement (between the two ST-Conv blocks) achieves the best result ($\text{MAE} = 3.2401 \pm 0.0108$). This ordering is consistent with the intuition that the attention module should operate on features that have already been spatially aggregated by the first ST-Conv block, yet still leave a second block to refine the attention-refined representation before output projection. Inserting attention too early (before spatial aggregation) or too late (after the final temporal convolution) either fails to exploit the global receptive field or disrupts the final feature refinement, both of which slightly increase error.

\paragraph{Number of Attention Heads} The multi-head variant ($h=4$) is the optimal configuration, outperforming both single-head ($h=1$, $\text{MAE} = 3.2501 \pm 0.0075$) and excessive heading ($h=8$, $\text{MAE} = 3.2494 \pm 0.0118$). Reducing to a single head limits the subspace diversity needed to capture multiple temporal scales, whereas increasing to eight heads adds capacity without further accuracy gains, confirming that $h=4$ strikes the best balance between representational diversity and optimization stability.

\paragraph{Necessity of Spatial Graph Modeling} Stripping the spatial Chebyshev graph convolution ($K=1$) increases overall MAE to $3.2488 \pm 0.0105$. Forecasting accuracy inherently relies on capturing the physical spatial network topology (e.g., traffic spillovers from upstream intersections), validating the necessity of spatial operators.

\paragraph{Channel Width and Efficiency Trade-offs} In terms of efficiency, reducing channels ($C=32$) significantly cuts parameters to 126K but worsens error to $3.2717 \pm 0.0057$. Interestingly, the $C=32$ variant exhibits a higher peak activation memory allocation (10,591.5 MB) during GPU execution compared to the canonical $C=80$ model (8,606.9 MB); this occurs because maintaining $h=4$ attention heads across a compact feature dimension induces additional tensor unrolling and PyTorch autograd allocation overhead during multi-head matrix multiplications. Overall, maintaining Depth=2 with $C=80$ retains 453K parameters and offers the optimal accuracy-efficiency balance.

\subsection{Robustness Analysis}
\label{sec:noise_robustness}

An inherent vulnerability of two-stage ITS architectures is \emph{two-stage error propagation}: because high-precision inductive loop sensors are absent across city-scale camera networks, Stage 2 graph forecasting models are trained on vehicle count sequences produced by Stage 1 vision perception backbones (which carry intrinsic visual estimation noise).

To rigorously evaluate how Stage 1 perception noise cascades into Stage 2 graph forecasting, we conduct a \textbf{Noise Sensitivity Analysis}. Rather than relying on arbitrary synthetic Gaussian noise, the noise injection parameters and evaluation levels ($\Delta \text{MAE}_{\text{noise}}$) are \textbf{strictly derived from the empirical error statistics of the Stage 1 vision models} evaluated across our 501 test camera feeds.

Specifically, empirical evaluation of Stage 1 backbones (Table~\ref{tab:stage1_density}) reveals two fundamental physical characteristics of camera-based vehicle counting under dense mixed traffic:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Asymmetric Undercounting Bias:} Under heavy congestion, severe inter-vehicle occlusions (motorcycles overlapping passenger cars) consistently bias the visual backbones toward \emph{undercounting} (as reflected by negative prediction bias across dense strata in Table~\ref{tab:stage1_density}).
    \item \textbf{Heteroscedastic Scaling:} Absolute counting error magnitudes scale proportionally with local node volume (higher vehicle density inherently incurs larger absolute error bounds).
\end{enumerate}

To capture these empirical properties, we model Stage 1 perception noise using a \emph{Heteroscedastic Asymmetric} Gamma process, where 80\% of injected noise terms are negative (matching the empirical undercounting bias) and the noise scale is proportional to node volume. We choose the Gamma distribution as the \emph{reference} noise model for three concrete reasons: (i) it is a positive, right-skewed magnitude law whose sign can be assigned separately, which matches the long-tailed, non-negative count-error magnitudes observed under congestion; (ii) its two parameters (shape, scale) give a closed-form, tractable sampler that we can calibrate exactly to each target $\Delta\text{MAE}$; and (iii) it is a standard, widely used reference for skewed positive magnitudes, so results are interpretable against prior work. We deliberately do \emph{not} claim it is uniquely correct: Weibull and log-normal alternatives are equally plausible right-skewed choices, and because the injected distribution is an experimenter-controlled modeling assumption---not a measured ground truth---our robustness conclusions are conditioned on it. Two design choices limit this conditioning in practice. First, the \emph{ranking} we rely on is distribution-agnostic in spirit: the four target $\Delta\text{MAE}$ levels are calibrated to the \emph{empirical} Stage~1 error statistics (minimum, mean, maximum, $+2\sigma$ stress), so any alternative distribution calibrated to the same $\Delta\text{MAE}$ targets probes the same four operating points rather than a different regime. Second, what the analysis attributes to TA-STGCN is a \emph{flatter degradation slope}, i.e., an ordering of error growth across levels, which depends on the model's response to perturbation severity more than on the exact tail shape of the perturbation. A formal sensitivity ablation that re-runs the four levels under matched Weibull and log-normal samplers is the correct way to confirm this, and we identify it as a priority for future work (Section~\ref{sec:deployment_limitations}); until then, the noise-robustness claim should be read as holding for the Gamma-calibrated reference, not as distribution-free. The target evaluation levels ($\Delta \text{MAE}_{\text{noise}}$) correspond directly to the empirical perception error distribution generated across individual test cameras in Stage 1:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Level 1 ($\Delta \text{MAE} = 3.49$):} Grounded in the minimum empirical camera counting error ($\text{MAE}_{\min} = 3.488 \approx 3.49$), representing optimal camera angles and unoccluded off-peak feeds.
    \item \textbf{Level 2 ($\Delta \text{MAE} = 4.64$):} Grounded in the mean empirical perception error ($\text{MAE}_{\text{mean}} = 4.640 \approx 4.64$) averaged across operational traffic camera conditions.
    \item \textbf{Level 3 ($\Delta \text{MAE} = 10.25$):} Grounded in the maximum empirical error bound ($\text{MAE}_{\max} = 10.250 \approx 10.25$) recorded at severely occluded, high-density rush-hour intersections (aligning with the large negative motorcycle bias observed under High density in Table~\ref{tab:stage1_density}).
    \item \textbf{Level 4 ($\Delta \text{MAE} = 13.95$):} A \emph{stress test} that extrapolates beyond the observed empirical error range ($\text{MAE}_{\max} + 2\sigma_{\text{MAE}} = 10.25 + 2 \times 1.849 \approx 13.95$). This level models a hypothetical compound-failure state rather than a directly observed camera condition; it is included solely to probe robustness at the tail of the error distribution and should be interpreted as a stress test, not as a measured operational scenario.
\end{itemize}

The implication for the contribution is direct: what the noise analysis shows is best stated as ``attention-based temporal modeling is substantially more robust to perception noise than non-attention graph/RNN modeling, and TA-STGCN is one attention-equipped architecture that realizes this; among attention-equipped models the differences are small.'' TA-STGCN's explicit model-level temporal attention with Chebyshev spatial convolutions does not demonstrably \emph{dominate} STAEformer or iTransformer under noise---its robustness is on the same order as theirs, while it remains substantially more robust than the non-attention graph baselines and far more parameter-economical than STAEformer. We therefore frame the noise result as a robustness \emph{property of the attention-equipped design class on this benchmark}, not as a victory of TA-STGCN over the SOTA attention baselines.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:deployment_limitations}

\subsection{Envisioned Edge-Cloud Architecture (Future Work)}
\label{sec:deployment_arch}

To motivate the deployment-oriented choices that the experiments \emph{do} evaluate (parameter economy, memory footprint, and count-only payloads), we briefly outline a prospective edge-cloud split. We stress that this is a \emph{design sketch for future work, not an experimental result}: none of the reported experiments measure device-level latency, energy use, bandwidth savings, or privacy guarantees, and the centralized pipeline used for all results is described in Section~\ref{sec:methods}.

In the envisioned deployment, Stage 1 (EfficientNet-B5 with mixed-precision quantization on edge runtimes such as OpenVINO/NCNN, following edge intelligence principles \cite{edge_intelligence, edge_its_survey}) would run per camera and emit only structured count payloads, while Stage 2 (TA-STGCN) would execute centrally on the aggregated counts. Transmitting a two-element count vector instead of a $\sim$500~KB 1080p snapshot gives an illustrative payload-size ratio of $\sim$$62{,}500\times$, though this excludes protocol overhead, retransmissions, and device measurements and is not an observed bandwidth result. Validating this architecture requires end-to-end measurements---latency, power, bandwidth, and privacy---across 608 devices, which we leave to future work.

\subsection{Parameter Economy vs. GPU Activation Memory}

A key hardware design insight from our benchmarks (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_5seeds}) is that static parameter count ($N_{\text{params}}$) alone is insufficient to evaluate model efficiency for city-scale graph architectures. While SOTA models like STAEformer maintain moderate parameter footprints ($\sim 568\text{K}$), their internal dynamic soft-attention allocations generate large activation footprints across $N=608$ nodes (\textbf{11,509.1 MB} peak GPU memory). The anomalously low footprint of iTransformer (265.2 MB) reflects its design: as an Inverted Transformer, it applies self-attention over individual variates/nodes rather than materializing an $N \times N$ spatial attention map, so it incurs no graph-scale activation memory; this is precisely why it is not a graph-structured baseline and is treated separately in our analysis.

In contrast, TA-STGCN explicitly decouples spatial Chebyshev graph convolutions from temporal self-attention. While this increases peak GPU memory by $77.9\%$ over the naive STGCN baseline ($4,837.6 \to 8,606.9$ MB), it still achieves a \textbf{25.2\% reduction} compared to STAEformer ($11,509.1$ MB). Furthermore, TA-STGCN achieves the lowest overall forecasting error ($\text{MAE} = \mathbf{3.2401 \pm 0.0108}$) with a \textbf{70.4\% parameter reduction} over heavy baselines like ASTGCN ($1,533\text{K} \to 453\text{K}$), establishing a competitive trade-off among parameter capacity, activation memory footprint, and multi-horizon forecasting accuracy for cost-constrained real-world deployment.

\subsection{Fairness of the Baseline Comparison}
We are explicit about three respects in which the Stage~2 comparison is a \emph{controlled implementation comparison} rather than a per-model-optimized contest, because each bears on how the numbers in Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_5seeds} should be read.

\textbf{Shared loss vs.\ native losses.} Every model is trained under the same Huber objective for experimental control. Some baselines were originally proposed with a different objective (e.g., GraphWaveNet uses an MAE-style loss), and retraining under Huber may not be their individually optimal setting. We accept this trade-off deliberately: applying a common objective holds the outlier-treatment constant and isolates architectural differences, but it means a given baseline's reported number is its Huber-trained result, not its published best. This is a reason to interpret absolute baseline accuracies as lower-bound estimates of their potential rather than as a ranking of the architectures themselves---and, correspondingly, a reason we do \emph{not} headline the small clean-input MAE margins, which sit inside the noise of such loss-choice effects.

\textbf{No per-model hyperparameter search.} We did not run an exhaustive, model-specific hyperparameter search on the 608-node benchmark; baselines use the authors' recommended configurations adapted to the shared input/output dimensions. Individual baselines may therefore be sensitive to further tuning, and their clean-input MAE could improve. This cuts in both directions: it means the comparison is conservative about declaring any architecture superior on clean inputs, which is consistent with our effect-size reading (all $|d|<0.1$) that the graph models are practically equivalent in point accuracy.

\textbf{iTransformer as a non-graph baseline.} iTransformer is included as a strong multivariate-time-series reference, but it is not a graph-structured forecaster: as an Inverted Transformer it applies self-attention over individual variates and never materializes an $N\times N$ spatial map (hence its 265.2~MB footprint). We therefore treat it separately throughout---it is excluded from ``graph-baseline'' statements, and where iTransformer is the closest competitor under noise (Level~4) we report the gap as a modest, non-decisive one rather than folding it into a graph-vs-graph claim. Its value in the table is to show that a non-graph attention model can also be noise-robust, which bounds how much of TA-STGCN's robustness is attributable to graph structure specifically versus to temporal attention in general.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
Several limitations remain in this study. First, the Stage 2 models are trained on vehicle count pseudo-labels produced by Stage 1, so forecasting fidelity is inherently bounded by visual perception error margins. Second, the total system computational cost is substantial. Although the Stage 2 graph model is lightweight (453K parameters), Stage 1 requires deploying a 28.3M-parameter EfficientNet-B5 per camera. Across a 608-node network, this entails 608 independent deployments of the same 28.3M-parameter model (over $17$ billion parameters in aggregate), posing significant edge-deployment, update, latency, and energy challenges. MobileNet-V3 (4.2M parameters) offers a smaller alternative, but its observed Stage 1 MAE is higher ($4.0126$ versus $3.6363$); quantization, distillation, and device-level throughput measurements are required before choosing a deployment backbone. Third, the Stage 1 benchmark compares only classification backbones under a direct-regression head and does not include specialized dense-counting methods (density-map estimators such as MCNN \cite{mcnn}, CSRNet \cite{csrnet}, and CAN \cite{can}; point-supervised counters such as P2PNet and DM-Count; or tracking-by-detection pipelines such as YOLO + ByteTrack \cite{yolo}). We therefore position direct regression as a \emph{practical trade-off} for this dataset rather than a claim of superiority over specialized counters: the aggregate count labels used here avoid the per-vehicle point/density annotation and per-camera calibration cost that such methods require, but a head-to-head comparison on matched annotations remains open. Fourth, the Stage 1 dataset contains only 5,012 images, which may limit generalizability across camera viewpoints; future studies should employ semi-supervised learning, additional annotation, and matched point-supervision for specialized counters. Fifth, the evaluation is constrained to a single city and a 3-month temporal window, without extreme seasonal or weather variation. Sixth, edge runtimes are conceptual: the experiments retrieve images centrally and do not measure latency, power, or end-to-end scale-out across 608 devices. Seventh, the Stage 2 benchmark covers a representative set of graph and Transformer baselines up to 2024 (STGCN \cite{stgcn}, GraphWaveNet \cite{wavenet}, ASTGCN \cite{astgcn}, MegaCRN \cite{megacrn}, STAEformer \cite{staeformer}, iTransformer \cite{itransformer}) but does not include the most recent 2024--2025 architectures, such as spatio-temporal state-space (Mamba) models, diffusion-based forecasters (e.g., CSDI, DiffSTG), or large-language-model and patch-based time-series methods (e.g., TimeXer, PatchTST, Crossformer). We did not integrate these newer families because they are not yet standardized for camera-sensed, category-preserving graph inputs and would require substantial re-engineering and per-model tuning on our 608-node benchmark. We note that the clean-input point-forecasting differences among the evaluated baselines are already practically negligible (Cohen's $d < 0.1$), so we do not expect the inclusion of additional recent architectures to change the central conclusion that the proposed framework's value lies in its camera-sensed benchmark, noise-robustness analysis, and deployment economy rather than in a marginal clean-input accuracy gain; nevertheless, a direct comparison with these newer families is an important direction for future work. Finally, the static RBF graph cannot adapt to non-physical dependencies or incident-driven structural shifts; adaptive graph learning and sensitivity tests for graph parameters are important future comparisons. In addition, the noise-robustness analysis uses a single (Gamma) reference distribution; a sensitivity ablation under matched Weibull and log-normal samplers, and per-baseline retraining under native losses, would further harden the comparison.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper introduced a two-stage framework for urban traffic monitoring and multi-horizon forecasting, evaluated on a real-world 608-node camera network in Ho Chi Minh City. The experimental pipeline combines camera-based vehicle perception (EfficientNet-B5) and graph forecasting (TA-STGCN); an edge-cloud realization is proposed for future deployment rather than implemented in the reported experiments.

Comprehensive evaluations show that TA-STGCN reaches competitive point-forecasting accuracy ($\text{MAE} = \mathbf{3.2401}$) with low run-to-run variation ($\text{CV}=0.33\%$); on clean inputs the evaluated graph models are practically equivalent in point accuracy. Under simulated perception noise, the robustness advantage \emph{stratifies by competitor class}: TA-STGCN degrades far more gracefully than the non-attention graph/RNN baselines (Cohen's $d$ up to $+5.84$ vs.\ GraphWaveNet), while against the attention-based baselines (STAEformer, iTransformer) its margin is only small-to-medium ($d \in [0.28, 0.67]$) and not a decisive separation---i.e., the robustness is a property of the attention-equipped design class on this benchmark, not a dominance of TA-STGCN over the SOTA attention baselines, with which it is comparably robust while being more parameter-economical (453K parameters, $25.2\%$ less peak training memory than STAEformer). The ablations and noise simulations indicate improved robustness under the evaluated perturbations, while leaving broader claims about optimal attention filtering, graph choice, noise-distribution sensitivity, and deployment performance for future validation.

The proposed edge-cloud architecture would transmit aggregate category-specific counts rather than images, but its privacy properties require deployment validation.
