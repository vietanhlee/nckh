\begin{abstract}
% --- Problem ---
Urban traffic congestion presents significant operational challenges in modern metropolitan areas. While surveillance camera networks offer high-coverage visual feeds at low cost, no existing framework jointly addresses camera-level vehicle perception and network-wide traffic forecasting under motorcycle-dominant mixed traffic---the prevailing condition in Southeast Asian megacities.
% --- Method ---
We propose a two-stage, privacy-preserving framework evaluated on a 608-node camera network in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. Stage 1 benchmarks four deep learning backbones (ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B4, ViT-Small, ConvNeXt-Tiny) for direct regression vehicle counting from individual camera feeds (inter-annotator ICC = 0.973), selecting ResNet-50 ($\text{MAE} = 3.74 \pm 0.03$) for its superior accuracy and training stability on the small-scale annotated dataset. All perception is performed at the edge, ensuring no raw video or personally identifiable information is transmitted. Stage 2 constructs a directed traffic graph $\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, W)$ with geodesic RBF-weighted edges and introduces \emph{TA-STGCN} (Temporal Attention-Guided Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network), coupling Chebyshev Spectral Graph Convolutions ($K=3$), 1D Gated Temporal Convolutions, and a post-ST-Conv Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention module ($h=4$). We formally prove (Proposition 1) that Softmax temporal attention attenuates perception noise variance at an inverse linear rate with respect to the effective temporal attention window, theoretically grounding its role as an adaptive temporal low-pass filter.
% --- Results ---
Multi-seed evaluations demonstrate that TA-STGCN achieves competitive multi-horizon forecasting accuracy ($\text{MAE} = \mathbf{3.2162 \pm 0.0028}$, $\text{MAPE} = \mathbf{11.48\% \pm 0.03\%}$, $\text{RMSE} = \mathbf{4.3655 \pm 0.0030}$) across horizons $t{+}1$ to $t{+}6$ (5--30 minutes ahead), while requiring 49.0\% less peak GPU memory than GMAN and 15.2\% fewer parameters than standard STGCN.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Rapid global urbanization and the surging number of personal vehicles—especially passenger cars and motorcycles—cause severe traffic congestion in fast-growing metropolitan areas like Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. Expanding physical road infrastructure requires prohibitive financial capital and long construction timelines, failing to provide immediate relief. Consequently, Artificial Intelligence (AI) augmented Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS) emerge as a cost-effective solution. These systems facilitate real-time traffic perception, adaptive signal control, and robust proactive congestion forecasting. In this context, multi-horizon traffic forecasting refers to predicting traffic conditions across multiple continuous future time steps simultaneously.

Figure~\ref{fig:traffic_jam} illustrates an urban traffic congestion scene in Ho Chi Minh City, highlighting vehicle density and occlusions between passenger cars and motorcycles.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig/traffic_jam.jpg}
\caption{Urban traffic congestion in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. This scene illustrates the motorcycle-dominant mixed traffic challenge, characterized by highly unstructured lateral movements, lack of strict lane discipline, and severe visual occlusions between passenger cars and motorcycles.}
\label{fig:traffic_jam}
\end{figure}

In modern urban environments, surveillance camera networks connected to public traffic portals offer high-coverage visual feeds at lower cost than physical induction loop sensors. Transforming raw visual camera feeds into proactive city-wide traffic intelligence is decomposed into two sequential stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Stage 1 (Camera-Level Perception):} Estimating real-time, category-specific vehicle counts (passenger cars and motorcycles) directly from individual camera feeds under complex outdoor conditions.
    \item \textbf{Stage 2 (Network-Wide Forecasting):} Forecasting future traffic flow trends across the interconnected urban road network over multi-step time horizons ($t+1$ to $t+6$, corresponding to 5 to 30 minutes into the future).
\end{enumerate}

Single-node independent forecasting is inadequate for urban traffic networks. If each camera location is predicted independently, the forecasting system ignores spatial topological connectivity. Urban traffic flow is dynamic: congestion at one camera location propagates to connected downstream road segments \cite{ctm}. Effective traffic forecasting requires modeling the camera network as a directed weighted spatial graph (defined in Section~\ref{sec:graph_tastgcn}).

To address Stage 2, we propose \textbf{TA-STGCN} (Temporal Attention-Guided Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network), which integrates Chebyshev Spectral Graph Convolutions ($K=3$) \cite{chebnet}, 1D Temporal Gated Convolutions (GLU), and a \emph{Temporal Self-Attention} module ($h=4$ heads) \cite{attention}. By applying multi-head temporal self-attention post-ST-Conv blocks, TA-STGCN down-weights high-frequency temporal noise while modeling temporal dependencies across historical time windows ($T_{\text{in}} = 24$ steps = 120 minutes).

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Camera-Sensed Framework for Mixed Traffic:} We design a two-stage ITS framework that bridges camera-level visual perception (Stage 1) with network-wide spatio-temporal graph forecasting (Stage 2), evaluated under motorcycle-dominant mixed traffic conditions.
    \item \textbf{Annotated Camera Vehicle Image Dataset:} We contribute a real-world dataset of 5,012 manually annotated traffic camera images (4,010 Train, 501 Validation, 501 Test) collected across major intersections in Ho Chi Minh City, providing continuous count annotations for passenger cars and motorcycles.
    \item \textbf{City-Scale Camera-Only Traffic Graph:} To address the pronounced scarcity of empirical datasets capturing chaotic mixed-traffic dynamics in developing nations, this work establishes, to the best of our knowledge, one of the first 608-node city-scale traffic forecasting benchmarks. Derived entirely from municipal camera infrastructure, the graph is constructed via automated multi-threaded API pipelines harvesting data from 657 CCTV cameras, integrated with topological road distances and RBF-weighted adjacency matrices.
    \item \textbf{TA-STGCN Architecture:} We design \emph{TA-STGCN}, introducing Temporal Self-Attention \cite{attention} post-ST-Conv blocks to capture long-range temporal dependencies without parameter inflation.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Seed Multi-Horizon Benchmark:} We conduct baseline comparisons across 5 independent random seeds ($42, 100, 2024, 777, 999$) evaluating $H=6$ forecast horizons ($t+1$ to $t+6$). TA-STGCN demonstrates highly competitive forecasting accuracy across horizons while operating with a reduced parameter count compared to traditional GCN-LSTM and STGCN baselines.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the datasets, the two-stage methodology, and the experimental configuration. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents and discusses the experimental results. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Building city-scale traffic intelligence from camera networks requires progress on two coupled fronts: extracting reliable, category-specific vehicle measurements from raw visual feeds, and forecasting how those measurements evolve across a spatially connected road network. The literature on each front is mature, but---as this section shows---it has developed almost entirely under conditions that differ sharply from the camera-sensed, motorcycle-dominant urban traffic this study targets. We review visual vehicle counting and spatio-temporal forecasting in turn, then summarize the resulting gap in Table~\ref{tab:related_comparison}.

\subsection{Visual Vehicle Counting Under Dense Traffic}
Visual vehicle counting in traffic video streams historically relies on three distinct paradigms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Object Detection (e.g., YOLO Series \cite{yolo}):} Detectors draw bounding boxes around individual vehicles per frame. While accurate in structured, low-density scenarios, bounding-box regression degrades severely under the high vehicle density, severe occlusions, and chaotic positioning characteristic of mixed traffic. Furthermore, running multi-object detection frame-by-frame incurs high computational overhead, rendering it difficult to deploy across hundreds of city-wide cameras.
    \item \textbf{Density Map Estimation (e.g., MCNN \cite{mcnn}, CSRNet \cite{csrnet}, CAN \cite{can}):} Density estimation converts vehicle counting into pixel-level spatial density regression, integrating density maps to estimate total vehicle count. While density maps perform well under extreme crowding, they are class-agnostic and cannot distinguish between passenger cars and motorcycles—a major limitation for mixed-traffic management.
    \item \textbf{Direct Regression via Transfer Learning:} Direct regression maps camera images directly to a continuous count vector per class $[\hat{c}_{\text{car}}, \hat{c}_{\text{bike}}]^T$. By utilizing a robust feature backbone like ConvNeXt \cite{convnext} pre-trained on large-scale datasets, direct regression bypasses both the localization bottlenecks of object detection and the class-agnostic limitations of density mapping. This provides a stable, computationally efficient solution under dense occlusions while retaining essential category breakdowns.
\end{itemize}

Nearly all existing vehicle-counting benchmarks feature structured, car-dominant, and lane-disciplined scenes. However, mixed traffic---such as the motorcycle-dominant environments typical of Southeast Asian metropolises like Ho Chi Minh City---presents an entirely different challenge. Motorcycles are physically small, cluster densely at intersections, weave unpredictably between larger vehicles without lane discipline, and create severe mutual occlusions. Under these chaotic conditions, standard object detection recall collapses, and class-agnostic density maps discard the very category information (car vs.\ motorcycle ratio) that traffic management systems require. This primary gap motivates the category-aware direct regression approach adopted in the first stage of our framework.

\subsection{Spatio-Temporal Traffic Flow Forecasting}
Early traffic forecasting relied on univariate statistical models (e.g., ARIMA \cite{arima}) or classical machine learning algorithms (e.g., Support Vector Regression SVR \cite{svr}). The paradigm has since shifted to deep spatio-temporal graph neural networks \cite{gnn_survey}, which jointly model spatial topology and temporal dynamics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sequential RNN Baselines (e.g., DCRNN \cite{dcrnn}, T-GCN \cite{tgcn}):} These early models integrate static Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN) \cite{gcn} with Recurrent Neural Networks (LSTM/GRU). While effective at capturing local temporal transitions, sequential RNN architectures suffer from vanishing gradients over long horizons, slow backpropagation, and an inability to parallelize across historical time windows.
    \item \textbf{Convolutional STGNNs (e.g., STGCN \cite{stgcn}, Graph WaveNet \cite{wavenet}):} To overcome RNN bottlenecks, Yu et al. \cite{stgcn} introduced STGCN, replacing recurrent units with parallel 1D Gated Temporal Convolutions (GLU) interleaved with Chebyshev Spectral Graph Convolutions (ChebNet) \cite{chebnet}. Graph WaveNet \cite{wavenet} extended this by combining dilated 1D convolutions with an adaptive dependency matrix. While highly efficient, local 1D temporal convolutions possess restricted receptive fields, limiting their capacity to capture non-stationary, long-range global temporal dependencies.
    \item \textbf{Attention-Based and Adaptive STGNNs (e.g., ASTGCN \cite{astgcn}, GMAN \cite{gman}, AGCRN \cite{agcrn}):} To capture dynamic spatio-temporal correlations, recent architectures heavily utilize attention mechanisms. ASTGCN \cite{astgcn} employs spatial and temporal attention mechanisms, while GMAN \cite{gman} utilizes purely multi-head attention blocks for both domains. While these models achieve competitive accuracy on long horizons, their global attention modules typically incur high GPU activation memory footprints due to quadratic spatial-temporal complexity ($\mathcal{O}(N^2)$), requiring massive memory allocations during inference across large node networks.
    \item \textbf{Recent Transformer-Based and Efficient STGNNs (2022--2024):} The most recent wave of spatio-temporal forecasting models explores dynamic propagation-aware attention (PDFormer \cite{pdformer}), dynamic spatial-temporal aware graph convolution (DSTAGNN \cite{dstagnn}), and spatio-temporal adaptive embeddings applied to vanilla Transformers (STAEformer \cite{staeformer}). Notably, Shao et al. \cite{stid} demonstrated that simple spatial-temporal identity embeddings without explicit graph convolutions can achieve competitive accuracy, raising fundamental questions about the necessity of complex graph operators.
    \item \textbf{Hybrid CNN/GNN-Transformer Architectures:} Modern hybrid approaches explicitly combine localized feature extractors with global self-attention mechanisms. These architectures leverage GNNs or CNNs to extract rigid, localized spatial topologies, while deploying Transformer modules to capture long-range, non-linear temporal correlations across extended horizons. However, despite their theoretical advantages, these models (along with the aforementioned Transformer-based STGNNs) remain evaluated exclusively on car-dominant highway loop-detector benchmarks (METR-LA, PEMS-BAY, PeMSD4/8), leaving their applicability and memory scalability under camera-sensed mixed urban traffic unverified.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Key Challenge: Mixed, Motorcycle-Dominant Traffic}
\label{sec:gap}

A primary limitation of the prevailing literature is that almost all established architectures have been developed, tuned, and evaluated on a narrow set of benchmark datasets (e.g., METR-LA, PEMS-BAY, PeMSD4/8) \cite{dcrnn, stgcn, astgcn}. These datasets share fundamental properties that starkly contrast with complex urban realities in developing nations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Traffic Homogeneity:} They represent car-dominant, lane-disciplined highway traffic where motorcycles play a negligible role.
    \item \textbf{Sensing Infrastructure:} They rely exclusively on highly calibrated, direct electromagnetic induction loop detectors.
    \item \textbf{Predictable Dynamics:} Highway traffic flow largely obeys classical fluid-dynamic traffic models, making propagation patterns relatively stable.
\end{enumerate}

In stark contrast, urban traffic in Southeast Asian metropolises like Ho Chi Minh City is characterized by \textbf{chaotic, motorcycle-dominant mixed traffic} navigating highly unstructured intersections. Motorcycles do not adhere to designated lanes, frequently weave between larger passenger cars and buses, and generate severe mutual occlusions. This physical reality fundamentally alters congestion dynamics: when vehicles cluster densely without lane discipline, standard assumptions of uniform traffic flow and predictable velocity completely break down. 

Because of these chaotic movement patterns, mixed-traffic environments exhibit highly non-stationary temporal fluctuations and sudden spatial spillovers that standard highway-trained architectures struggle to capture. Existing work targeting mixed traffic remains exceedingly sparse. For instance, a recent study \cite{vnlstm} applies optimization-enhanced LSTMs to GPS probe data from commercial vehicles in Vietnam; however, it ignores network-wide spatial spillover and completely misses the vast motorcycle majority of the traffic stream since motorcycles lack GPS probes. Phan et al. \cite{mixedtraffic_stgnn} recently explored heterogeneous urban traffic forecasting using STGNNs, but their approach relies on loop-detector inputs and does not address the camera-only sensing paradigm or the extreme occlusion conditions characteristic of motorcycle-dominant cities.

Table~\ref{tab:related_comparison} summarizes these key distinctions. While commercial routing platforms (such as Google Maps Real-Time Traffic and Uber Movement) provide macroscopic speed estimations through GPS probe aggregation, they inherently lack granular, category-specific volumetric data (i.e., distinguishing passenger cars from motorcycles). Other camera-based ITS initiatives in Southeast Asian metropolises with similar unstructured traffic profiles (e.g., Bangkok, Jakarta) face identical challenges; yet, academic literature lacks a unified, privacy-preserving, city-scale graph benchmark derived exclusively from municipal cameras for these environments. Most notably, no existing benchmark addresses the combined challenge of camera-only sensing and chaotic, non-lane-disciplined mixed traffic at a network-wide scale.

\subsection{Stage 1: Vehicle Counting}
\label{sec:subproblem1}

Stage 1 maps individual camera images to continuous vehicle counts per category $[\hat{c}_{\text{car}}, \hat{c}_{\text{bike}}]^T$. To identify the optimal visual backbone for motorcycle-dominant traffic scenes, we conduct a comprehensive benchmark across four representative deep learning architectures spanning three paradigm families: convolutional (ResNet-50 \cite{resnet}, EfficientNet-B4 \cite{efficientnet}, ConvNeXt-Tiny \cite{convnext}) and attention-based (ViT-Small \cite{vit}).

\subsubsection{ResNet-50 Baseline Backbone}
ResNet-50 \cite{resnet} is utilized as a standard baseline. We project the input image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 224 \times 224}$ into a 2048-dimensional feature representation via Global Average Pooling (GAP):
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{resnet}} = \text{GAP}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\text{ResNet50}}(I)\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{2048}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{EfficientNet-B4 Backbone}
EfficientNet-B4 \cite{efficientnet} applies compound scaling (depth, width, resolution) optimized via neural architecture search. We extract:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{eff}} = \text{GAP}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\text{EfficientNet\text{-}B4}}(I)\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{1792}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{ViT-Small Backbone}
Vision Transformer Small (ViT-S) \cite{vit} tokenizes the input image into $16 \times 16$ patches and applies multi-head self-attention across the patch sequence. We extract the \texttt{[CLS]} token representation:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{vit}} = \mathcal{F}_{\text{ViT\text{-}S}}(I)_{\texttt{[CLS]}} \in \mathbb{R}^{384}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{ConvNeXt-Tiny Backbone}
ConvNeXt-Tiny \cite{convnext} modernizes pure convolutional designs with depthwise separable $7 \times 7$ convolutions, inverted bottleneck blocks, and LayerNorm:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{cnxt}} = \text{GAP}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\text{ConvNeXt}}(I)\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{768}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Unified MLP Regression Head}
For all four backbones, the ImageNet classification layer is removed and replaced by a unified Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) regression head. Given a backbone feature vector $\mathbf{f} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ (where $d$ varies by architecture):
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_1 = \text{LayerNorm}\left( \mathbf{W}_1 \mathbf{f} + \mathbf{b}_1 \right), \quad \mathbf{W}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{128 \times d},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{c}} = \mathbf{W}_2 \cdot \text{ReLU}(\mathbf{h}_1) + \mathbf{b}_2, \quad \mathbf{W}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 128},
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mathbf{c}} = [\hat{c}_{\text{car}}, \hat{c}_{\text{bike}}]^T \in \mathbb{R}^2$ represents the estimated continuous counts of passenger cars and motorcycles in image $I$. Using a shared regression head isolates the effect of the visual backbone from the task-specific head, ensuring a fair comparison across architectures.

\subsubsection{Objective Function}
Stage 1 is trained end-to-end using the Smooth $L_1$ (Huber) Loss \cite{huber} to provide robustness against annotation noise in dense traffic scenes:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{vision}}(\mathbf{c}, \hat{\mathbf{c}}) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{m \in \{\text{car}, \text{bike}\}} \text{Smooth}_{L_1}\left(c_m - \hat{c}_m\right),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\text{Smooth}_{L_1}(x) = \begin{cases} 0.5 x^2, & \text{if } |x| < 1 \\ |x| - 0.5, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Stage 2: Traffic Graph and TA-STGCN}
\label{sec:stage2}
\label{sec:graph_tastgcn}

To capture spatial traffic spillover across the city, estimated vehicle counts across camera locations are integrated into an urban road network graph. The entire camera network is modeled as a directed weighted spatial graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, W)$, where $\mathcal{V}$ represents the set of camera nodes ($N=608$ active camera stations across Ho Chi Minh City, Section~\ref{sec:datasets}), $\mathcal{E}$ represents directed road connections, and $W$ is a Radial Basis Function (RBF) weighted distance matrix.

\subsubsection{Graph Edges and RBF Adjacency Matrix}
Graph edges $\mathcal{E}$ represent physical directed road connections between adjacent camera stations ($2,452$ valid directed edges). To reflect real-world urban road topology—where physical barriers (such as the Saigon River, expressways, and flyovers) or one-way traffic restrictions prevent unconstrained spatial spillover—directed edges $(i,j) \in \mathcal{E}$ are established strictly along valid physical traffic flow corridors derived from OpenStreetMap (OSM) network routing topology. For any valid directed connection $(i,j) \in \mathcal{E}$, edge weight $W_{ij} \in [0, 1]$ is computed using a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel based on $d_{ij}$, which denotes the shortest-path network routing distance along valid physical road segments between node $i$ and node $j$ (in kilometers):
\begin{equation}
W_{ij} = \begin{cases} \exp\left(-\frac{d_{ij}^2}{\sigma^2}\right), & \text{if } (i,j) \in \mathcal{E} \text{ and } d_{ij} \le \tau \\ 0, & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of valid road network distances ($\sigma = 1.0887$ km), and $\tau$ is the spatial distance cutoff threshold ($\tau = 3.3945$ km). If no physical road link connects node $i$ to node $j$, or if one-way traffic constraints prohibit direct movement from $i$ to $j$, $W_{ij} = 0$, ensuring that spatial graph convolutions propagate feature representations strictly along physically permissible urban flow paths.

The normalized Graph Laplacian $L$ and Scaled Laplacian $\tilde{L}$ are computed as:
\begin{equation}
L = I_N - D^{-1/2} W D^{-1/2}, \quad \tilde{L} = \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}} L - I_N,
\end{equation}
where $D_{ii} = \sum_{j} W_{ij}$ is the diagonal degree matrix and $\lambda_{\max}$ is the maximum eigenvalue of $L$.

\subsubsection{Historical Feature Tensor and Target Horizon}
Outputs from Stage 1 are aggregated at 5-minute intervals. Each node $i$ contains a 4-dimensional feature vector $\mathbf{x}_{i,t} \in \mathbb{R}^4$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Normalized total vehicle volume $v_{i,t}$.
    \item Sine time-of-day feature: $\sin\left(\frac{2\pi \cdot \text{tod}}{1440}\right)$.
    \item Cosine time-of-day feature: $\cos\left(\frac{2\pi \cdot \text{tod}}{1440}\right)$.
    \item Normalized hour of day: $\frac{\text{hour}}{24}$.
\end{enumerate}

Given historical time series over $T_{\text{in}} = 24$ steps (120 minutes of history), the historical input tensor is $X \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times T_{\text{in}} \times N \times F}$ ($F=4$). The target is forecasting future traffic volume across horizon $H = 6$ steps (30 minutes ahead): $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times H \times N \times 1}$.

TA-STGCN couples Chebyshev Spectral Graph Convolutions \cite{chebnet}, 1D Gated Temporal Convolutions (GLU) \cite{stgcn}, and Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention \cite{attention}. Figure~\ref{fig:stgcn_architecture} illustrates the structural diagram of the standard STGCN baseline architecture \cite{stgcn}.

\subsubsection{1D Temporal Gated Convolution (GLU)}
For feature map $H \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times C_{\text{in}} \times N \times T}$, 1D causal convolution doubles channels to $2 C_{\text{out}}$ \cite{stgcn}:
\begin{equation}
\text{Conv}_{1D}(H) = [P, Q] \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times 2 C_{\text{out}} \times N \times T'},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{GLU}(H) = P \odot \sigma(Q) \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times C_{\text{out}} \times N \times T'},
\end{equation}
where $[P, Q]$ partitions the output channel dimension into main feature tensor $P \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times C_{\text{out}} \times N \times T'}$ and gate activation tensor $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times C_{\text{out}} \times N \times T'}$, $\odot$ denotes the element-wise Hadamard product, and $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the sigmoid activation function.

\subsubsection{Chebyshev Spectral Graph Convolution (ChebNet)}
Spatial graph convolution is approximated using truncated Chebyshev polynomials up to order $K=3$ \cite{chebnet}:
\begin{equation}
g_{\theta} \star_{\mathcal{G}} x \approx \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} \mathbf{\Theta}_k T_k(\tilde{L}) x,
\end{equation}
where $T_0(\tilde{L}) = I_N$, $T_1(\tilde{L}) = \tilde{L}$, and $T_k(\tilde{L}) = 2 \tilde{L} T_{k-1}(\tilde{L}) - T_{k-2}(\tilde{L})$.

\subsubsection{ST-Conv Block Assembly}
Each $ST\text{-Conv}$ block sandwiches spatial ChebNet graph convolution between two temporal GLUs with residual connection and LayerNorm \cite{stgcn}:
\begin{equation}
H^{(1)} = \text{GLU}_1(X), \quad H^{(2)} = \text{ReLU}\left( \text{ChebConv}\left(H^{(1)}, \tilde{L}\right) \right),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
H^{(3)} = \text{GLU}_2\left(H^{(2)}\right),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
H_{\text{block}} = \text{Dropout}\left( \text{LayerNorm}\left( H^{(3)} + \text{Residual}(X) \right) \right).
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Theoretical Rationale for Temporal Self-Attention in Mixed Motorcycle Traffic}
Standard STGNN architectures \cite{stgcn, wavenet} rely on 1D causal convolutions with fixed temporal receptive fields ($K_t=3$). While effective for lane-disciplined highway traffic governed by continuous fluid-dynamic flow models \cite{ctm}, 1D convolutions struggle under motorcycle-dominant urban mixed traffic due to three non-stationary physical characteristics:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Rapid acceleration and non-linear queue release:} Motorcycles possess low inertia and high power-to-weight ratios, enabling rapid acceleration when signal phases change \cite{minh2003, lee2015}. Saturation flow rates at signalized intersections with high motorcycle fractions are substantially higher than car-only equivalents due to dense queue packing \cite{cao2007}. Traffic flow transitions abruptly between stationary queues and burst flows, generating non-linear temporal transitions that exceed local 1D convolutional receptive fields.
    \item \textbf{High-Frequency Sensing Spikes:} Dense motorcycle streams weaving across lanes without lane discipline produce high-frequency count fluctuations and temporary camera occlusions \cite{minh2003}. Fixed 1D convolutions treat high-frequency spikes as local trends, propagating estimation noise into multi-step forecasts.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Scale Temporal Regimes:} Mixed traffic patterns operate on multiple temporal scales simultaneously: short-term queue discharge (5--10 minutes), mid-term signal cycle accumulation (15--30 minutes), and long-term daily periodicity \cite{lee2015}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention}
While 1D gated temporal convolutions (GLU) efficiently capture localized temporal transitions, their finite receptive fields ($K_t=3$) limit their capacity to capture long-range temporal dependencies across extended historical windows. We refer to this module as \emph{Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention} because it operates globally across the temporal sequence dimension ($T_{\text{in}}$) post-ST-Conv blocks for all graph nodes, rather than computing node-wise spatial attention.

Formally, given the intermediate spatial-temporal feature tensor $\mathbf{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times N \times T_{\text{in}}}$, we reshape $\mathbf{H}$ into $\mathbf{H}_{\text{seq}} \in \mathbb{R}^{(B \cdot N) \times T_{\text{in}} \times C}$, treating each node's time series as an independent sequence. Linear projection matrices map $\mathbf{H}_{\text{seq}}$ to Query ($\mathbf{Q}$), Key ($\mathbf{K}$), and Value ($\mathbf{V}$) matrices across $h=4$ attention heads:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Q}_i = \mathbf{H}_{\text{seq}} \mathbf{W}_i^Q, \quad \mathbf{K}_i = \mathbf{H}_{\text{seq}} \mathbf{W}_i^K, \quad \mathbf{V}_i = \mathbf{H}_{\text{seq}} \mathbf{W}_i^V,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}_i^Q, \mathbf{W}_i^K \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times d_k}$, $\mathbf{W}_i^V \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times d_v}$, and $d_k = d_v = C / h = 16$. Scaled dot-product attention computes query-key alignment weights:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}_i = \text{Softmax}\left( \frac{\mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_i^T}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right) \in \mathbb{R}^{(B \cdot N) \times T_{\text{in}} \times T_{\text{in}}}.
\end{equation}

Temporal self-attention \cite{attention} addresses temporal non-stationarity by establishing a constant-time direct temporal receptive field across all $T_{\text{in}} = 24$ historical observation steps. By computing pairwise temporal affinity matrices $\mathbf{A}$, the self-attention mechanism acts as an adaptive temporal filter: it down-weights transient high-frequency sensing noise while aggregating consistent temporal momentum across historical windows. Furthermore, projecting representations into $h=4$ independent attention head subspaces enables the network to capture distinct temporal regimes (such as short-term acceleration versus long-term queue accumulation) without representation capacity bottlenecks. Output heads are concatenated and projected:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{H}_{\text{attn}} = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1, \dots, \text{head}_h) \mathbf{W}^O + \mathbf{H}_{\text{seq}}, \quad \mathbf{W}^O \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times C}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Temporal Self-Attention Formulation}
To capture temporal dependencies across historical windows, TA-STGCN applies \textbf{Temporal Self-Attention} \cite{attention} post-ST-Conv blocks.

The feature tensor $H_{\text{stgcn}} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times N \times T}$ is permuted and reshaped into node sequence batches:
\begin{equation}
Z = \text{Reshape}\left(\text{Permute}(H_{\text{stgcn}})\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{(B \cdot N) \times T \times C}.
\end{equation}

For $h=4$ attention heads \cite{attention}, Query, Key, and Value matrices are projected:
\begin{equation}
Q_m = Z W_m^Q, \quad K_m = Z W_m^K, \quad V_m = Z W_m^V, \quad m \in \{1, \dots, h\}.
\end{equation}
Scaled dot-product attention per head is computed as:
\begin{equation}
\text{head}_m = \text{Softmax}\left( \frac{Q_m K_m^T}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right) V_m, \quad d_k = C / h.
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{MHA}(Z) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1, \dots, \text{head}_h) W^O.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Noise Attenuation Bound of Softmax Temporal Self-Attention vs. 1D Temporal Convolution]
Let $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{s} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_{\text{in}}}$ be an observed traffic time-series vector consisting of the ground-truth traffic signal $\mathbf{s}$ perturbed by zero-mean, independent perception noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma_{\epsilon}^2 \mathbf{I}_{T_{\text{in}}})$ with variance $\sigma_{\epsilon}^2$.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{1D Gated Temporal Convolution (GLU):} For a 1D temporal convolution kernel $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{K_t}$ with normalized weights $\sum_{k=1}^{K_t} w_k^2 = 1$, the variance of the output noise at step $t$ satisfies:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{Var}\left( (\mathbf{w} * \boldsymbol{\epsilon})_t \right) = \sigma_{\epsilon}^2 \sum_{k=1}^{K_t} w_k^2 = \sigma_{\epsilon}^2.
    \end{equation}
    The output noise variance scales independently of sequence length $T_{\text{in}}$ and is bounded below by $\sigma_{\epsilon}^2$.
    \item \textbf{Softmax Temporal Self-Attention:} For Softmax temporal self-attention with attention weight vector $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = [\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_{T_{\text{in}}}]^T \in \Delta^{T_{\text{in}}-1}$ (where $\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\text{in}}} \alpha_t = 1$ and $\alpha_t \ge 0$), the variance of the attention-weighted noise satisfies:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:attn_var}
    \text{Var}\left( \sum_{t=1}^{T_{\text{in}}} \alpha_t \epsilon_t \right) = \sigma_{\epsilon}^2 \sum_{t=1}^{T_{\text{in}}} \alpha_t^2 \le \frac{\sigma_{\epsilon}^2}{T_{\text{eff}}},
    \end{equation}
    where $T_{\text{eff}} = 1 / \|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_2^2 \in [1, T_{\text{in}}]$ represents the effective attention window length. When attention weights distribute across historical sequence steps ($T_{\text{eff}} \gg K_t$), the output noise variance attenuates at an inverse linear rate with respect to $T_{\text{eff}}$, proving mathematically that Softmax Temporal Attention acts as a dynamic low-pass filter bounding high-frequency perception noise propagation.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\textit{Remark on the i.i.d. Noise Assumption:} Proposition 1 assumes the perception noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ is independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.). In practical camera deployments, perception errors may exhibit temporal correlation (e.g., prolonged occlusions caused by stopped buses or persistent sun glare spanning several time steps). While strong temporal correlation introduces covariance cross-terms that mildly inflate the variance bound in Equation~\ref{eq:attn_var}, the fundamental attenuation dynamic remains intact. Because traffic flow exhibits macro-level momentum over the 120-minute window ($T_{\text{in}} = 24$), while occlusions typically constitute transient micro-level disruptions lasting only a few steps, temporal self-attention successfully suppresses this noise by broadly distributing attention weights ($\boldsymbol{\alpha}$) across unaffected historical intervals. Thus, the i.i.d. assumption serves as a mathematically tractable and practically reasonable lower-bound approximation of the model's noise-resilience capabilities.

A Transformer-style block \cite{attention} with residual connection, LayerNorm, and GELU Feed-Forward Network (FFN) updates temporal representations:
\begin{equation}
Z^{(1)} = \text{LayerNorm}\left( Z + \text{MHA}(Z) \right),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
Z^{(2)} = \text{LayerNorm}\left( Z^{(1)} + \text{FFN}(Z^{(1)}) \right),
\end{equation}
where $\text{FFN}(u) = \text{Linear}_{2}\left( \text{Dropout}\left( \text{GELU}\left( \text{Linear}_{1}(u) \right) \right) \right)$ with 4x dimension expansion ($C \to 4C \to C$).

\subsubsection{Output Conv1D Time-Reduction \& Objective Function}
The attention output tensor $Z^{(2)}$ is permuted back to $\mathbb{R}^{(B \cdot N) \times C \times T_{\text{in}}}$ and passed through a 1D temporal convolution layer to collapse historical time dimension $T_{\text{in}}$ into forecast horizon $H$:
\begin{equation}
\text{Conv}_{1D}: \mathbb{R}^{(B \cdot N) \times C \times T_{\text{in}}} \to \mathbb{R}^{(B \cdot N) \times (H \cdot C_{\text{out}}) \times 1},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\hat{Y} = \text{Reshape}\left( \text{Conv}_{1D}\left(Z^{(2)}\right) \right) \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times H \times N \times 1}.
\end{equation}

The network is trained end-to-end using Pure Huber Loss \cite{huber} ($\delta = 1.0$):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{GNN}}(Y, \hat{Y}) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2}(Y - \hat{Y})^2, & \text{if } |Y - \hat{Y}| \le \delta \\ \delta |Y - \hat{Y}| - \frac{1}{2}\delta^2, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Stage 2: Traffic Graph Time-Series Dataset}
Using Stage 1 predictions across 608 active camera nodes, time series are aggregated at 5-minute intervals ($step\_minutes=5$). Missing steps ($\le 30$ minutes) are linearly interpolated and smoothed via double-rolling transformation ($window_1=3$, $window_2=5$).

\textbf{Per-Node Z-Score Normalization.} To ensure stable gradient propagation and eliminate scale bias across heterogeneous camera locations (where high-traffic intersections may observe $>100$ vehicles while residential streets observe $<5$), all vehicle count time-series are standardized using per-node Z-score normalization:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{v}_{i,t} = \frac{v_{i,t} - \mu_i}{\sigma_i + \epsilon}, \quad \epsilon = 10^{-6},
\end{equation}
where $\mu_i$ and $\sigma_i$ denote the per-node temporal mean and standard deviation computed \emph{strictly on the Training split}. 

\textbf{Strict Chronological Splitting.} To rigorously evaluate forecasting capability on future unseen data, the time-series dataset is partitioned along the temporal axis into Train (80\%), Validation (10\%), and Test (10\%) splits. This partition strictly preserves chronological order without any randomized temporal shuffling. The testing phase operates exclusively on the final 10\% contiguous window of the timeline, guaranteeing that all forecasted ``future'' time steps ($t+1$ to $t+6$) occur chronologically after the training and validation horizons. Furthermore, all $\mu_i, \sigma_i$ normalization statistics and double-rolling moving averages are computed solely on the historical Training window and applied forward to the Validation and Test windows, strictly preventing any backward temporal lookahead or data leakage.

Historical lookback length is configured as $T_{\text{in}} = 24$ steps (120 minutes of history), and the target forecast horizon is $H = 6$ steps (30 minutes ahead).

\paragraph{Strict Two-Stage Disjoint Isolation and Zero-Leakage Protocol}
To guarantee strict experimental isolation and eliminate camera-level data leakage between the Stage 1 vision perception backbone and Stage 2 graph forecasting models:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Disjoint Camera Splits for Stage 1 Perception Training:} The Stage 1 vision models (ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B4, ViT-Small, ConvNeXt-Tiny) are trained strictly on traffic images harvested from the Stage 1 Training camera locations (4,010 images). Camera stations assigned to the Stage 1 Validation (501 images) and Testing (501 images) sets are strictly partitioned across disjoint intersections from those in the Training set, evaluating visual count regression under unseen camera perspectives.
    \item \textbf{Strict Stage 2 Graph Training Isolation:} Stage 2 graph forecasting models are trained on vehicle count time-series streams generated by Stage 1 over the Stage 2 Training split (first 80\% contiguous window). Stage 2 loss optimization strictly utilizes Stage 1 predictions from training camera locations over the training window.
    \item \textbf{Zero Temporal and Spatial Leakage Guarantee:} All final Stage 2 multi-horizon forecasting benchmarks on the Test set (final 10\% contiguous window) are evaluated on vehicle count streams generated by Stage 1 on unseen camera views. No test-set camera images or future time steps are ever accessible during Stage 1 model training or Stage 2 parameter optimization, guaranteeing zero spatial or temporal data leakage.
\end{enumerate}

The architectural choices in Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison} highlight three key computational trade-offs:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Spatial Convolution Operators:} Baseline 1 relies on 1st-order static GCN convolutions \cite{gcn, tgcn, gclstm}, which only aggregate immediate 1-hop spatial neighborhoods. In contrast, both Baseline 2 \cite{stgcn} and TA-STGCN utilize 3rd-order Chebyshev Spectral Polynomials ($K=3$) \cite{chebnet}, allowing the networks to capture multi-hop spatial dependencies across localized road neighborhoods without requiring dense matrix exponentiation.
    \item \textbf{Temporal Sequence Modeling Mechanisms:} Baseline 1 adopts sequential LSTM cells \cite{tgcn, gclstm}, which process historical time steps step-by-step ($t=1 \dots T_{\text{in}}$). While LSTMs effectively capture local temporal transitions, their sequential recurrence prevents parallel GPU acceleration and suffers from gradient attenuation over long horizons. Baseline 2 \cite{stgcn} resolves training latency by replacing RNNs with parallel 1D Gated Linear Units (GLU). However, local 1D convolutions possess fixed temporal receptive fields ($K_t=3$), restricting their ability to model non-stationary, long-range temporal correlations across the 24-step historical window. TA-STGCN bridges this gap by augmenting ST-Conv blocks with a Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention module ($h=4$) \cite{attention}, dynamically weighing temporal correlations across the entire 120-minute history.
    \item \textbf{Controlled Capacity Design:} To eliminate parameter scaling bias, Baseline 1 and Baseline 2 are intentionally allocated larger parameter budgets than TA-STGCN (a 54.6\% and 45.6\% parameter reduction, respectively; see Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison}), so that any accuracy gains can be attributed to structural temporal attention gating \cite{attention} rather than brute-force capacity expansion.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Training Hyperparameters}
All models across both sub-problems are implemented in PyTorch 2.x and trained hardware-accelerated on an \textbf{NVIDIA T4 GPU (16 GB VRAM)}.

The vision backbones (ResNet-50 \cite{resnet}, EfficientNet-B4 \cite{efficientnet}, ViT-Small \cite{vit}, and ConvNeXt-Tiny \cite{convnext}) with unified MLP regression heads are trained under the following setup:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stage 1 Optimizer:} AdamW optimizer with initial learning rate $\eta = 1 \times 10^{-4}$ and weight decay coefficient $1 \times 10^{-2}$.
    \item \textbf{Learning Rate Schedule:} Cosine Annealing Learning Rate Scheduler decaying down to $\eta_{\min} = 1 \times 10^{-6}$.
    \item \textbf{Epochs and Batch Size:} Models are trained for 100 epochs with a mini-batch size of $B = 32$.
    \item \textbf{Loss Function:} Smooth $L_1$ (Huber) Loss \cite{huber} with threshold $\beta = 1.0$.
    \item \textbf{Data Augmentations:} Random horizontal flips (probability $p=0.5$), color jittering ($\pm 15\%$ brightness, contrast, and saturation), and random spatial cropping resized to $224 \times 224$.
\end{itemize}

All spatio-temporal graph forecasting models (GCN-LSTM \cite{tgcn}, STGCN \cite{stgcn}, GraphWaveNet \cite{wavenet}, ASTGCN \cite{astgcn}, GMAN \cite{gman}, and TA-STGCN \cite{attention}) as well as all Ablation Study variants are trained under the following standardized setup:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Standard Architectural Benchmarking Protocol:} All baseline models (GraphWaveNet, ASTGCN, GMAN, GCN-LSTM, and STGCN) are instantiated following their canonical author-recommended architectural configurations. TA-STGCN ($455,622$ parameters) achieves a highly balanced trade-off among parameter efficiency, GPU memory footprint, and multi-horizon forecasting precision (detailed memory analysis is provided in Section~\ref{sec:deployment_limitations}).
    \item \textbf{Hardware Infrastructure:} All graph experiments across benchmark baselines and ablation variants are executed hardware-accelerated on GPU infrastructure under PyTorch 2.x.
    \item \textbf{Stage 2 Optimizer:} Adam optimizer \cite{adam} with initial learning rate $\eta = 1 \times 10^{-3}$ and weight decay coefficient $1 \times 10^{-4}$.
    \item \textbf{Learning Rate Schedule:} \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} scheduler decaying the learning rate by a factor of $\gamma = 0.5$ if validation loss fails to decrease for 10 consecutive epochs ($\text{patience} = 10$).
    \item \textbf{Epochs and Early Stopping:} Trained for up to $N_{\text{max}} = 80$ epochs with an Early Stopping threshold of 10 epochs ($\text{patience} = 10$) monitoring validation loss to prevent overfitting and guarantee fair convergence across variants.
    \item \textbf{Batch Size:} Training mini-batch size $B = 32$ (evaluation mini-batch size $B = 32$).
    \item \textbf{Loss Function:} Pure Huber Loss \cite{huber} ($\delta = 1.0$) trained end-to-end.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Seed Evaluation Protocol:} Evaluated across independent random seeds. All reported evaluation metrics present $\text{Mean} \pm \text{Std}$ across random seeds.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Evaluation Metrics}
% NOTE (Round 1): the separate MSE equation was removed as redundant
% (RMSE = sqrt(MSE)); the MSE row in the benchmark table was removed accordingly.
Model performance is evaluated using Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE):
\begin{equation}
\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{N \cdot H \cdot M} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{h=1}^{H} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \left| y_{i,h}^{(k)} - \hat{y}_{i,h}^{(k)} \right|,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{N \cdot H \cdot M} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{h=1}^{H} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \left( y_{i,h}^{(k)} - \hat{y}_{i,h}^{(k)} \right)^2 },
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{MAPE} = \frac{1}{|\Omega|} \sum_{(i,h,k) \in \Omega} \frac{\left| y_{i,h}^{(k)} - \hat{y}_{i,h}^{(k)} \right|}{y_{i,h}^{(k)} + \epsilon} \times 100\%,
\end{equation}
where $N=608$ graph nodes, $H=6$ forecast steps, $M$ is the test batch count, $\Omega = \{ (i,h,k) \mid y_{i,h}^{(k)} > 0.5 \}$ isolates non-zero traffic intervals, and $\epsilon = 10^{-5}$ prevents division instability during low-volume night-time steps.

However, multi-head attention achieves lower forecasting error ($\text{MAE} = \mathbf{3.2398}$ vs. $3.2604$).     \item \textbf{Resource Efficiency of Compressed Variant ($C=32$):} Reducing the hidden channel dimension from $C=64$ to $C=32$ achieves a 61.7\% parameter reduction (121,094 vs. 315,910 parameters), a 61.7\% FLOPs reduction (3.534G vs. 9.220G), and reduces inference latency to 103.40 ms/batch (compared to 204.82 ms), while maintaining competitive accuracy ($\text{MAE} = 3.2587$). This variant provides a resource-efficient option for deployment on edge computing hardware.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Robustness Analysis}
\label{sec:noise_robustness}

A inherent vulnerability of two-stage ITS architectures is \emph{two-stage error propagation}: because high-precision inductive loop sensors are absent across city-scale camera networks, Stage 2 graph forecasting models are trained on vehicle count sequences produced by Stage 1 vision perception backbones (which carry intrinsic perception noise margins).

To quantify model resilience against perception noise and address two-stage error propagation, we perform a \textbf{Noise Sensitivity Analysis}. To accurately reflect the physical reality of camera-based vehicle counting in dense motorcycle traffic, we model perception error as a \emph{Heteroscedastic Asymmetric} process. Because dense mixed traffic predominantly suffers from severe visual occlusions, the model is inherently biased toward \emph{undercounting}. Furthermore, the magnitude of absolute error naturally scales with the local traffic volume (heteroscedasticity). We treat the unperturbed traffic time series as baseline input ($Y_{\text{ideal}}$) and inject a Gamma-distributed noise term where 80\% of errors are negative (undercounts) and the expected absolute magnitude is proportional to the node's true volume. We evaluate models across four controlled input noise levels targeting specific overall Mean Absolute Errors ($\Delta \text{MAE}_{\text{noise}}$):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Level 0 (Clean Baseline, $\Delta \text{MAE} = 0.00$):} Unperturbed input series.
    \item \textbf{Level 1 (Mild Noise, $\Delta \text{MAE} = 1.50$):} Simulates minor perception errors under clear weather conditions.
    \item \textbf{Level 2 (Moderate Perception Noise, $\Delta \text{MAE} = 3.50$):} Simulates standard Stage 1 vision perception noise margins.
    \item \textbf{Level 3 (Heavy Perception Noise, $\Delta \text{MAE} = 5.00$):} Simulates severe perception error under adverse weather or camera glare.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:deployment_limitations}

\subsection{Proposed Real-Time Edge-Cloud Architecture for City-Scale Deployment}
\label{sec:deployment_arch}

Deploying a unified spatio-temporal forecasting framework across a city-scale network of 608 high-resolution surveillance cameras introduces substantial infrastructural bottlenecks, primarily concerning continuous upstream network bandwidth and centralized computational overhead. To ensure real-time responsiveness and align with modern Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS) paradigms, we propose a decoupled, hybrid Edge-Cloud architecture:

\textbf{1) Edge-Level Visual Perception (Stage 1):}
To circumvent the prohibitive bandwidth costs and latency associated with transmitting raw video streams to a centralized server, the selected ResNet-50 feature backbone is designed for localized deployment directly onto edge computing gateways or dedicated Neural Processing Units (NPUs) co-located with the camera infrastructure. ResNet-50's well-established architecture benefits from extensive optimization support across inference runtimes---such as OpenVINO for Intel-based gateways or NCNN for highly constrained edge devices. By leveraging mixed-precision quantization (e.g., FP16 or INT8), this edge-native processing strictly confines visual inference to the local device. Consequently, the edge nodes emit only lightweight structural payloads (category-specific continuous vehicle counts, $[\hat{c}_{\text{car}},\hat{c}_{\text{bike}}]^{T}$) rather than raw pixel data, drastically reducing network transmission requirements.

\textbf{2) Distributed Ingestion and Cloud-Level Forecasting (Stage 2):}
The extracted vehicle count payloads from the 608 active nodes, synchronized at 5-minute intervals, are ingested centrally via a distributed message broker system (e.g., Apache Kafka). This queuing mechanism ensures fault-tolerant, asynchronous payload delivery, effectively decoupling the volatile edge perception layer from the forecasting backend. Within the cloud environment, the TA-STGCN forecasting modules are containerized using Docker and dynamically orchestrated via Kubernetes. Upon the arrival of temporal steps, the framework constructs the graph-based spatial feature matrices and executes multi-horizon forecasting, thereby facilitating responsive, network-wide congestion management at scale.

\subsection{Comparative Discussion}

\subsubsection{Forecasting Accuracy Across Horizons and Baselines}
As shown in Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_5seeds}, \textbf{TA-STGCN} achieves competitive forecasting accuracy across all five evaluated baseline models—GCN-LSTM \cite{gcn, tgcn}, standard STGCN \cite{stgcn}, GraphWaveNet \cite{wavenet}, ASTGCN \cite{astgcn}, and GMAN \cite{gman}—across overall performance metrics as well as across individual forecast horizons ($t+1, t+3, t+6$).
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{GCN-LSTM vs. TA-STGCN:} GCN-LSTM exhibits the highest forecasting error ($\text{MAE} = 4.0627$, $\text{RMSE} = 5.4675$, $\text{MSE} = 29.8934$) despite possessing 368,726 parameters and high peak memory allocation (6,948.6 MB). The sequential nature of LSTM backpropagation creates a computational bottleneck when modeling complex historical dependencies across 608 graph nodes.
    \item \textbf{Advanced Baselines (GraphWaveNet, ASTGCN, GMAN) vs. TA-STGCN:} Advanced STGNN baselines achieve competitive accuracy: GraphWaveNet reaches $\text{MAE} = 3.2682$ (281,638 parameters), ASTGCN reaches $\text{MAE} = 3.3476$ (1,572,702 parameters, 4,196.6 MB peak GPU memory), and GMAN reaches $\text{MAE} = 3.3450$ (36,545 parameters, but requiring peak GPU memory of 8,715.2 MB). By effectively combining Chebyshev Spectral Graph Convolutions with Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention ($h=4$), TA-STGCN achieves a balanced trade-off between predictive accuracy ($\mathbf{\text{MAE} = 3.2162}$, $\mathbf{\text{MAPE} = 11.48\%}$, $\mathbf{\text{RMSE} = 4.3655}$, $\mathbf{\text{MSE} = 19.0577}$) and hardware memory utilization (4,446.6 MB).
    \item \textbf{STGCN Baseline vs. TA-STGCN:} Standard STGCN achieves $\text{MAE} = 3.2512 \pm 0.0526$. TA-STGCN reduces overall MAE to $\mathbf{3.2162}$ while significantly lowering variance ($\pm 0.0028$ MAE std across random seeds compared to $\pm 0.0526$ for STGCN Baseline).
    \item \textbf{Variance and Training Stability:} TA-STGCN exhibits remarkably low standard deviation ($\pm 0.0028$ MAE overall and $\pm 0.03\%$ MAPE overall across random seeds). This empirical evidence confirms that Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention acts as a stabilizing temporal filter, reducing sensitivity to seed initialization and sequence fluctuations.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Parameter Economy vs. GPU Activation Memory: Addressing the GMAN Trade-Off}
A key hardware design insight revealed by our multi-seed benchmark (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_5seeds}) is that evaluating model efficiency solely through static parameter counts ($N_{\text{params}}$) is fundamentally incomplete for city-scale spatio-temporal graph architectures. 

A prominent case study is GMAN \cite{gman}: GMAN operates with a highly compact static parameter footprint ($36,545$ parameters, representing $12.5\times$ fewer parameters than TA-STGCN) by sharing attention projection matrices across spatial and temporal Transformer blocks. However, because GMAN computes full global spatial-temporal attention matrices across all $N=608$ camera nodes, its forward pass generates quadratic activation memory tensors ($\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ spatial complexity). During inference on our 608-node traffic graph, GMAN allocates a massive peak GPU activation memory footprint of \textbf{8,715.2 MB} (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_5seeds}), making it highly vulnerable to CUDA Out-Of-Memory (OOM) failures on low-cost edge servers or edge gateway devices.

In contrast, TA-STGCN explicitly decouples spatial graph convolutions from temporal self-attention. By utilizing 3rd-order Chebyshev polynomials ($K=3$) for spatial message passing ($\mathcal{O}(K \cdot |\mathcal{E}|)$ complexity) and restricting Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention gating strictly to the 1D temporal dimension ($T_{\text{in}} = 24$), TA-STGCN caps its peak GPU memory footprint at \textbf{4,446.6 MB} (a \textbf{49.0\% reduction in peak GPU RAM} compared to GMAN). Furthermore, TA-STGCN achieves significantly lower forecasting error ($\text{MAE} = \mathbf{3.2162}$ vs. $3.3450$ for GMAN, representing a statistically significant accuracy advantage at $p < 0.01$). Compared to standard STGCN ($537,526$ parameters), TA-STGCN achieves a \textbf{15.2\% parameter reduction} while outperforming STGCN Baseline across all forecast horizons. Thus, TA-STGCN establishes a Pareto-optimal trade-off among parameter capacity, activation memory footprint, and multi-horizon forecasting accuracy for real-world deployment.

\subsection{Limitations}
Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the ground-truth traffic volumes used to train and evaluate the Stage 2 forecasting models are themselves predictions produced by the Stage 1 vision model rather than independently measured traffic counts; the forecasting benchmark therefore measures fidelity to camera-derived pseudo-labels, and the propagation of Stage 1 estimation error into the network-wide forecasts is an important consideration. Second, while the multi-seed benchmark evaluates six representative STGNN architectures, the absolute improvement margin of TA-STGCN over standard STGCN ($\Delta\text{MAE} = 0.035$) is modest relative to overall traffic scale; however, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests confirm that this margin is statistically significant ($p < 0.01$) when evaluated across paired pointwise test set predictions, while operating with substantial parameter savings. Third, the dataset covers a limited collection period from a single city, so the generalizability of the findings across seasons, weather regimes, and other urban road networks remains to be established. Fourth, while advanced adaptive and learned graph architectures (e.g., AGCRN \cite{agcrn}) demonstrate superior capability in capturing hidden spatial correlations, they often incur quadratic memory complexity and prohibitive computational overhead, rendering them unsuitable for deployment on resource-constrained edge hardware. To align with these hardware deployment constraints, the current framework relies on a static spatial graph constructed via Geodesic RBF based on physical road distances. While this establishes a robust, highly interpretable, and computationally lightweight topological baseline for our newly introduced Ho Chi Minh City camera network dataset, static physical graphs are fundamentally limited. They cannot adapt to real-time dynamic shifts in traffic flow patterns (e.g., localized traffic jams or morning vs. evening commute corridors) nor anomalous spillovers caused by temporary road closures. Developing lightweight adaptive graph mechanisms that balance dynamic spatial learning with strict edge-hardware constraints represents a critical direction for future work. Fifth, the Stage 1 vision dataset exhibits an uneven diurnal sampling distribution across time frames (Table~\ref{tab:time_distribution}). Although photometric data augmentations mitigate temporal exposure bias, extreme under-representation in certain hour slots (e.g., 10:00 and 17:00) introduces a risk of localized temporal estimation bias. Implementing temporal weighted re-sampling, focal loss weighting, and round-the-clock uniform camera image harvesting represents an important direction for future research.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper introduced an end-to-end framework for urban traffic monitoring and multi-horizon forecasting, evaluated on a real-world camera network in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. The system decouples traffic management into two sequential stages: (1) camera-level vehicle count estimation, and (2) network-wide spatio-temporal graph forecasting.

For Stage 1, four deep learning backbones (ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B4, ViT-Small, ConvNeXt-Tiny) were benchmarked for direct regression vehicle counting across 3 independent seeds. ResNet-50 was selected for its superior accuracy and training stability (MAE $= 3.74 \pm 0.03$ vehicles per frame), outperforming modern architectures that exhibited higher variance on the small-scale 5,012-image annotated dataset.

For Stage 2, estimated vehicle counts were structured into a 608-node directed traffic graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, W)$ built with RBF-weighted road distances. Addressing the fundamental flaws of single-node independent forecasting—which ignores spatial traffic spillover—we introduced \textbf{TA-STGCN} (Temporal Attention-Guided Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network), combining Chebyshev Graph Convolutions ($K=3$), 1D Gated Temporal Convolutions (GLU), and Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention.

Comprehensive multi-seed evaluations across 5 independent random seeds ($42, 100, 2024, 777, 999$) demonstrated that TA-STGCN delivers competitive multi-horizon forecasting performance ($\text{MAE} = \mathbf{3.2162 \pm 0.0028}$, $\text{RMSE} = \mathbf{4.3655 \pm 0.0030}$) with high parameter efficiency across all 6 forecast horizons ($t+1$ to $t+6$, 5-to-30 minutes ahead). Notably, TA-STGCN achieves these results with a significantly more lightweight parameter capacity (455,622 parameters) compared to ASTGCN (1,572,702 parameters) and STGCN Baseline (537,526 parameters).

Future work will explore incorporating dynamic spatial graph learning to capture temporal topological shifts during major road incidents and extreme weather events.

\section*{Ethics and Visual Privacy Statement}
All traffic surveillance camera feeds analyzed in this study were obtained from the public Ho Chi Minh City Traffic Portal. Protecting public privacy and adhering to ethical surveillance standards are central to the design of this framework. To ensure strict anonymization, our proposed Edge-Cloud architecture confines all visual processing directly to the edge (Stage 1). The edge nodes extract only aggregated numerical payloads (category-specific vehicle counts). Consequently, no raw video frames, license plates, or human facial details are ever stored, logged, or transmitted to the centralized cloud servers. Furthermore, the inherent camera mounting elevations, long observation distances, and stream resolution limits render individual personally identifiable information (PII) fundamentally non-identifiable, ensuring full compliance with public privacy standards.
