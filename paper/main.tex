\begin{abstract}
Urban traffic congestion presents major operational and socio-economic challenges in modern metropolises. While surveillance camera networks offer accessible, high-coverage visual feeds, transforming raw video streams into reliable network-wide traffic forecasts requires solving two coupled sub-problems: (1) camera-level visual vehicle count estimation, and (2) network-wide multi-horizon traffic flow forecasting. In this paper, we present a unified framework integrating visual perception and spatio-temporal graph modeling over a real-world urban road network in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. For Sub-problem 1, a ConvNeXt-Tiny backbone with a direct regression head estimates continuous counts of cars and motorbikes directly from camera feeds (trained on 4,010 images, validated on 501, and tested on 501 images). For Sub-problem 2, we construct a 608-node directed traffic graph $\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, W)$ built from Ho Chi Minh City Traffic Portal cameras with RBF-weighted road distances. Addressing the fundamental flaws of single-node independent forecasting—which ignores dynamic traffic spillover—we introduce \emph{TA-STGCN} (Temporal Attention-Guided Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network), an architecture coupling Chebyshev Spectral Graph Convolutions ($K=3$), 1D Gated Temporal Convolutions (GLU), and Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention. Comprehensive evaluations across independent random seeds demonstrate that TA-STGCN achieves superior multi-step forecasting accuracy ($\text{MAE} = \mathbf{3.2162 \pm 0.0028}$, $\text{MAPE} = \mathbf{11.48\% \pm 0.03\%}$, $\text{RMSE} = \mathbf{4.3655 \pm 0.0030}$) across prediction horizons ($t+1$ to $t+6$, 5-to-30 minutes ahead), outperforming traditional GCN-LSTM, standard STGCN, GraphWaveNet, ASTGCN, and GMAN baselines while maintaining high training stability and parameter efficiency.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Rapid global urbanization and the exponential growth of personal motor vehicles—particularly passenger cars and motorcycles—have led to severe urban traffic congestion in developing metropolises, such as Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. Constructing new physical road infrastructure requires substantial financial capital and extended timeframes, offering minimal immediate relief. Consequently, Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS) powered by Artificial Intelligence (AI) have become an essential, cost-effective strategy for real-time traffic perception, adaptive signal control, and proactive congestion forecasting.

Figure~\ref{fig:traffic_jam} illustrates a representative urban traffic congestion scene in Ho Chi Minh City, highlighting extreme vehicle density and complex occlusions between passenger cars and motorcycles.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig/traffic_jam.jpg}
\caption{Urban traffic congestion in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam, illustrating extreme vehicle density and severe occlusions between passenger cars and motorcycles.}
\label{fig:traffic_jam}
\end{figure}

In modern urban environments, surveillance camera networks connected to public traffic portals offer high-coverage visual feeds at a fraction of the cost of physical induction loop sensors. However, transforming raw visual camera feeds into proactive city-wide traffic intelligence presents a complex multi-faceted challenge. To address this effectively, the overall urban traffic management framework is decomposed into two sequential sub-problems:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sub-problem 1 (Camera-Level Perception):} Estimating real-time, category-specific vehicle counts (passenger cars and motorbikes) directly from individual camera feeds under complex outdoor conditions.
    \item \textbf{Sub-problem 2 (Network-Wide Forecasting):} Forecasting future traffic flow trends across the entire interconnected urban road network over multi-step time horizons ($t+1$ to $t+6$, corresponding to 5 to 30 minutes into the future).
\end{enumerate}

A critical methodological insight of this study is that \textbf{single-node independent forecasting is fundamentally inadequate for urban traffic networks}. If each camera location is treated as an isolated univariate time series and predicted independently (e.g., fitting a separate model per road segment), the forecasting system completely ignores spatial topological connectivity. Urban traffic flow is non-stationary and dynamic: congestion at one camera location rapidly spills over and propagates to connected downstream road segments via physical road connections and traffic bottlenecks \cite{ctm}. Predicting traffic at a single node without accounting for spatial graph topology leads to severe error propagation, particularly during peak hours or sudden traffic incidents. Effective traffic forecasting therefore requires modeling the entire camera network as a directed weighted spatial graph (formally defined in Section~\ref{sec:graph_tastgcn}).

To address Sub-problem 2 and overcome the receptive field limitations of prior graph architectures \cite{stgcn, astgcn, stsgcn}, we propose \textbf{TA-STGCN} (Temporal Attention-Guided Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network), which integrates Chebyshev Spectral Graph Convolutions ($K=3$) \cite{chebnet}, 1D Temporal Gated Convolutions (GLU), and a \emph{Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention} module ($h=4$ heads) \cite{attention}. By applying multi-head temporal self-attention post-ST-Conv blocks, TA-STGCN functions as a dynamic low-pass filter that effectively down-weights high-frequency non-stationary temporal noise while modeling multi-aspect temporal dependencies (immediate momentum, hourly periodicity, and daily patterns) across historical time windows ($T_{\text{in}} = 24$ steps = 120 minutes).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Building city-scale traffic intelligence from camera networks requires progress on two coupled fronts: extracting reliable, category-specific vehicle measurements from raw visual feeds, and forecasting how those measurements evolve across a spatially connected road network. The literature on each front is mature, but---as this section shows---it has developed almost entirely under conditions that differ sharply from the camera-sensed, motorcycle-dominant urban traffic this study targets. We review visual vehicle counting and spatio-temporal forecasting in turn, then summarize the resulting gap in Table~\ref{tab:related_comparison}.

\subsection{Visual Vehicle Count Estimation}
Visual vehicle count estimation from surveillance cameras has evolved through three main computational paradigms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Object Detection (e.g., YOLO Series \cite{yolo}):} Detectors draw bounding boxes around individual vehicles per frame. While highly accurate in structured, low-density scenarios, bounding-box regression degrades severely under the high vehicle density, severe occlusions, and chaotic positioning characteristic of mixed traffic. Furthermore, running multi-object detection frame-by-frame incurs high computational overhead, rendering it difficult to deploy across hundreds of city-wide cameras.
    \item \textbf{Density Map Estimation (e.g., MCNN \cite{mcnn}, CSRNet \cite{csrnet}, CAN \cite{can}):} Fully Convolutional Networks map images to pixel-wise density maps, which are then integrated to yield a total vehicle count. Although highly robust to crowd-level occlusions, density mapping inherently struggles to distinguish between distinct vehicle classes (e.g., separating motorcycles from passenger cars) and requires labor-intensive, pixel-level point annotations for training.
    \item \textbf{Direct Regression via Transfer Learning:} Direct regression maps camera images directly to a continuous count vector per class $[\hat{c}_{\text{car}}, \hat{c}_{\text{bike}}]^T$. By utilizing a robust feature backbone like ConvNeXt \cite{convnext} pre-trained on large-scale datasets, direct regression bypasses both the localization bottlenecks of object detection and the class-agnostic limitations of density mapping. This provides a highly stable, computationally efficient solution under dense occlusions while retaining essential category breakdowns.
\end{itemize}

Nearly all existing vehicle-counting benchmarks feature structured, car-dominant, and lane-disciplined scenes. However, mixed traffic---such as the motorcycle-dominant environments typical of Southeast Asian metropolises like Ho Chi Minh City---presents an entirely different challenge. Motorcycles are physically small, cluster densely at intersections, weave unpredictably between larger vehicles without lane discipline, and create severe mutual occlusions. Under these chaotic conditions, standard object detection recall collapses, and class-agnostic density maps discard the very category information (car vs.\ motorbike ratio) that traffic management systems require. This fundamental gap motivates the category-aware direct regression approach adopted in the first stage of our framework.

\subsection{Spatio-Temporal Traffic Flow Forecasting}
Early traffic forecasting relied on univariate statistical models (e.g., ARIMA \cite{arima}) or classical machine learning algorithms (e.g., Support Vector Regression SVR \cite{svr}). The paradigm has since shifted to deep spatio-temporal graph neural networks \cite{gnn_survey}, which jointly model spatial topology and temporal dynamics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sequential RNN Baselines (e.g., DCRNN \cite{dcrnn}, T-GCN \cite{tgcn}):} These early models integrate static Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN) \cite{gcn} with Recurrent Neural Networks (LSTM/GRU). While effective at capturing local temporal transitions, sequential RNN architectures suffer from vanishing gradients over long horizons, slow backpropagation, and an inability to parallelize across historical time windows.
    \item \textbf{Convolutional STGNNs (e.g., STGCN \cite{stgcn}, Graph WaveNet \cite{wavenet}):} To overcome RNN bottlenecks, Yu et al. \cite{stgcn} introduced STGCN, replacing recurrent units with parallel 1D Gated Temporal Convolutions (GLU) interleaved with Chebyshev Spectral Graph Convolutions (ChebNet) \cite{chebnet}. Graph WaveNet \cite{wavenet} extended this by combining dilated 1D convolutions with an adaptive dependency matrix. While highly efficient, local 1D temporal convolutions possess restricted receptive fields, limiting their capacity to capture non-stationary, long-range global temporal dependencies.
    \item \textbf{Attention-Based and Adaptive STGNNs (e.g., ASTGCN \cite{astgcn}, GMAN \cite{gman}, AGCRN \cite{agcrn}):} To capture dynamic spatio-temporal correlations, recent architectures heavily utilize attention mechanisms. ASTGCN \cite{astgcn} employs spatial and temporal attention mechanisms, while GMAN \cite{gman} utilizes purely multi-head attention blocks for both domains. While these models often achieve state-of-the-art accuracy on long horizons, their complex attention modules typically incur a massive parameter footprint and quadratic computational complexity, making them difficult to deploy in resource-constrained environments.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Key Challenge: Mixed, Motorcycle-Dominant Traffic}
\label{sec:gap}

A critical limitation of the prevailing literature is that almost all established architectures have been developed, tuned, and evaluated on a narrow set of benchmark datasets (e.g., METR-LA, PEMS-BAY, PeMSD4/8) \cite{dcrnn, stgcn, astgcn}. These datasets share fundamental properties that starkly contrast with complex urban realities in developing nations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Traffic Homogeneity:} They represent car-dominant, lane-disciplined highway traffic where motorcycles play a negligible role.
    \item \textbf{Sensing Infrastructure:} They rely exclusively on highly calibrated, direct electromagnetic induction loop detectors.
    \item \textbf{Predictable Dynamics:} Highway traffic flow largely obeys classical fluid-dynamic traffic models, making propagation patterns relatively stable.
\end{enumerate}

In stark contrast, urban traffic in Southeast Asian metropolises like Ho Chi Minh City is characterized by \textbf{chaotic, motorcycle-dominant mixed traffic} navigating highly unstructured intersections. Motorcycles do not adhere to designated lanes, frequently weave between larger cars and buses, and generate severe mutual occlusions. This physical reality fundamentally alters congestion dynamics: when vehicles cluster densely without lane discipline, standard assumptions of uniform traffic flow and predictable velocity completely break down. 

Because of these chaotic movement patterns, mixed-traffic environments exhibit highly non-stationary temporal fluctuations and sudden spatial spillovers that standard highway-trained architectures struggle to capture. Existing work targeting mixed traffic remains exceedingly sparse. For instance, a recent study \cite{vnlstm} applies optimization-enhanced LSTMs to GPS probe data from commercial vehicles in Vietnam; however, it ignores network-wide spatial spillover and completely misses the vast motorcycle majority of the traffic stream since motorcycles lack GPS probes.

Table~\ref{tab:related_comparison} summarizes these critical distinctions. Most notably, no existing benchmark addresses the combined challenge of camera-only sensing and chaotic, non-lane-disciplined mixed traffic at a network-wide scale.

\label{sec:methods}

This section describes the study area and datasets, followed by the mathematical formulations and system components for both sub-problems: Sub-problem 1 (Camera-Level Vehicle Count Estimation via ConvNeXt-Tiny \cite{convnext} vs. ResNet-50 \cite{resnet}) and Sub-problem 2 (Network-Wide Spatio-Temporal Graph Forecasting via TA-STGCN \cite{stgcn, attention}).

\subsubsection{Camera Network Acquisition}
We harvest surveillance camera feeds from the Ho Chi Minh City Traffic Portal. Initially, 657 camera feeds are acquired along with Camera IDs, location names, and spatial coordinates (Latitude, Longitude). An automated multi-threaded API downloader with HTTP session pooling scans all 608 cameras every 60 seconds, building a synchronized, time-aligned sequence of traffic images. Figure~\ref{fig:api_samples} shows representative traffic camera feeds collected from the Ho Chi Minh City Traffic Portal API across major urban intersections.

\subsection{Sub-Problem 1: Camera-Level Vehicle Count Estimation}
\label{sec:subproblem1}

Sub-problem 1 maps individual camera images to continuous vehicle counts per category $[\hat{c}_{\text{car}}, \hat{c}_{\text{bike}}]^T$. We compare two deep convolutional feature backbones: the baseline ResNet-50 \cite{resnet} and the proposed ConvNeXt-Tiny \cite{convnext}.

\subsubsection{ResNet-50 Baseline Backbone}
ResNet-50 \cite{resnet} is utilized as a standard baseline. We project the input image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 224 \times 224}$ into a 2048-dimensional feature representation via Global Average Pooling (GAP):
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{resnet}} = \text{GAP}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\text{ResNet50}}(I)\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{2048}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{ConvNeXt-Tiny Feature Backbone (Proposed)}
For robust visual feature extraction, we adopt the standard ConvNeXt-Tiny architecture \cite{convnext}. Retaining the original unmodified backbone configuration, we extract a compact, high-level visual representation $\mathbf{f}_{\text{visual}} \in \mathbb{R}^{768}$ via Global Average Pooling:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{visual}} = \text{GAP}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\text{ConvNeXt}}(I)\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{768}.
\end{equation}

Figure~\ref{fig:convnext_counting_arch} illustrates the pipeline of the proposed ConvNeXt-Tiny vehicle count estimation model, showcasing the original backbone followed by the custom MLP regression head.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig/ConvNext_tiny_coutin.png}

\subsubsection{Custom MLP Regression Head}
For both backbones, the ImageNet classification layer is removed and replaced by a custom Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) regression head:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_1 = \text{LayerNorm}\left( \mathbf{W}_1 \mathbf{f}_{\text{visual}} + \mathbf{b}_1 \right), \quad \mathbf{W}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{128 \times 768},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{c}} = \mathbf{W}_2 \cdot \text{ReLU}(\mathbf{h}_1) + \mathbf{b}_2, \quad \mathbf{W}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 128},
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mathbf{c}} = [\hat{c}_{\text{car}}, \hat{c}_{\text{bike}}]^T \in \mathbb{R}^2$ represents the estimated continuous counts of passenger cars and motorbikes in image $I$.

\subsubsection{Objective Function}
Sub-problem 1 is trained end-to-end using the Smooth $L_1$ (Huber) Loss \cite{huber} to provide robustness against annotation noise in dense traffic scenes:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{vision}}(\mathbf{c}, \hat{\mathbf{c}}) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{m \in \{\text{car}, \text{bike}\}} \text{Smooth}_{L_1}\left(c_m - \hat{c}_m\right),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\text{Smooth}_{L_1}(x) = \begin{cases} 0.5 x^2, & \text{if } |x| < 1 \\ |x| - 0.5, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Sub-Problem 2: Traffic Graph Construction and TA-STGCN}
\label{sec:graph_tastgcn}

To capture spatial traffic spillover across the city, estimated vehicle counts across camera locations are integrated into an urban road network graph. The entire camera network is modeled as a directed weighted spatial graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, W)$, where $\mathcal{V}$ represents the set of camera nodes ($N=608$ active camera stations across Ho Chi Minh City, Section~\ref{sec:datasets}), $\mathcal{E}$ represents directed road connections, and $W$ is a Radial Basis Function (RBF) weighted distance matrix.

\subsubsection{Graph Edges and RBF Adjacency Matrix}
Graph edges $\mathcal{E}$ represent physical road connections between adjacent camera stations, totaling 2,452 valid edges in the network. Edge weights $W_{ij} \in [0, 1]$ are computed using a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel based on $d_{ij}$, which denotes the actual travel distance between node $i$ and node $j$ (in kilometers):
\begin{equation}
W_{ij} = \begin{cases} \exp\left(-\frac{d_{ij}^2}{\sigma^2}\right), & \text{if } i \neq j \text{ and } d_{ij} \le \tau \\ 0, & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of the valid road distances ($\sigma = 1.0887$ km), and $\tau$ is the spatial distance cutoff threshold. To induce adjacency matrix sparsity while preserving significant local spatial dependencies, $\tau$ can be empirically set to $2.6790$ km (90th percentile) or $3.3945$ km (95th percentile), compared to the maximum observed network distance of $6$ km.

The normalized Graph Laplacian $L$ and Scaled Laplacian $\tilde{L}$ are computed as:
\begin{equation}
L = I_N - D^{-1/2} W D^{-1/2}, \quad \tilde{L} = \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}} L - I_N,
\end{equation}
where $D_{ii} = \sum_{j} W_{ij}$ is the diagonal degree matrix and $\lambda_{\max}$ is the maximum eigenvalue of $L$.

\subsubsection{Historical Feature Tensor and Target Horizon}
Outputs from Sub-problem 1 are aggregated at 5-minute intervals. Each node $i$ contains a 4-dimensional feature vector $\mathbf{x}_{i,t} \in \mathbb{R}^4$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Normalized total vehicle volume $v_{i,t}$.
    \item Sine time-of-day feature: $\sin\left(\frac{2\pi \cdot \text{tod}}{1440}\right)$.
    \item Cosine time-of-day feature: $\cos\left(\frac{2\pi \cdot \text{tod}}{1440}\right)$.
    \item Normalized hour of day: $\frac{\text{hour}}{24}$.
\end{enumerate}

Given historical time series over $T_{\text{in}} = 24$ steps (120 minutes of history), the historical input tensor is $X \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times T_{\text{in}} \times N \times F}$ ($F=4$). The target is forecasting future traffic volume across horizon $H = 6$ steps (30 minutes ahead): $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times H \times N \times 1}$.

TA-STGCN couples Chebyshev Spectral Graph Convolutions \cite{chebnet}, 1D Gated Temporal Convolutions (GLU) \cite{stgcn}, and Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention \cite{attention}. Figure~\ref{fig:stgcn_architecture} illustrates the structural diagram of the standard STGCN baseline architecture \cite{stgcn}.

\subsubsection{1D Temporal Gated Convolution (GLU)}
For feature map $H \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times C_{\text{in}} \times N \times T}$, 1D causal convolution doubles channels to $2 C_{\text{out}}$ \cite{stgcn}:
\begin{equation}
\text{Conv}_{1D}(H) = [P, Q] \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times 2 C_{\text{out}} \times N \times T'},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{GLU}(H) = P \odot \sigma(Q) \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times C_{\text{out}} \times N \times T'},
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ denotes element-wise product and $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the sigmoid gate.

\subsubsection{Chebyshev Spectral Graph Convolution (ChebNet)}
Spatial graph convolution is approximated using truncated Chebyshev polynomials up to order $K=3$ \cite{chebnet}:
\begin{equation}
g_{\theta} \star_{\mathcal{G}} x \approx \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} \mathbf{\Theta}_k T_k(\tilde{L}) x,
\end{equation}
where $T_0(\tilde{L}) = I_N$, $T_1(\tilde{L}) = \tilde{L}$, and $T_k(\tilde{L}) = 2 \tilde{L} T_{k-1}(\tilde{L}) - T_{k-2}(\tilde{L})$.

\subsubsection{ST-Conv Block Assembly}
Each $ST\text{-Conv}$ block sandwiches spatial ChebNet graph convolution between two temporal GLUs with residual connection and LayerNorm \cite{stgcn}:
\begin{equation}
H^{(1)} = \text{GLU}_1(X), \quad H^{(2)} = \text{ReLU}\left( \text{ChebConv}\left(H^{(1)}, \tilde{L}\right) \right),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
H^{(3)} = \text{GLU}_2\left(H^{(2)}\right),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
H_{\text{block}} = \text{Dropout}\left( \text{LayerNorm}\left( H^{(3)} + \text{Residual}(X) \right) \right).
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention}
To capture dynamic long-range temporal dependencies across the 24-step historical window, TA-STGCN applies \textbf{Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention} \cite{attention} at the model output stage (post ST-Conv blocks).

The feature tensor $H_{\text{stgcn}} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times N \times T}$ is permuted and reshaped into node sequence batches:
\begin{equation}
Z = \text{Reshape}\left(\text{Permute}(H_{\text{stgcn}})\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{(B \cdot N) \times T \times C}.
\end{equation}

For $h=4$ attention heads \cite{attention}, Query, Key, and Value matrices are projected:
\begin{equation}
Q_m = Z W_m^Q, \quad K_m = Z W_m^K, \quad V_m = Z W_m^V, \quad m \in \{1, \dots, h\}.
\end{equation}
Scaled dot-product attention per head is computed as:
\begin{equation}
\text{head}_m = \text{Softmax}\left( \frac{Q_m K_m^T}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right) V_m, \quad d_k = C / h.
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{MHA}(Z) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1, \dots, \text{head}_h) W^O.
\end{equation}

A Transformer-style block \cite{attention} with residual connection, LayerNorm, and GELU Feed-Forward Network (FFN) updates temporal representations:
\begin{equation}
Z^{(1)} = \text{LayerNorm}\left( Z + \text{MHA}(Z) \right),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
Z^{(2)} = \text{LayerNorm}\left( Z^{(1)} + \text{FFN}(Z^{(1)}) \right),
\end{equation}
where $\text{FFN}(u) = \text{Linear}_{2}\left( \text{Dropout}\left( \text{GELU}\left( \text{Linear}_{1}(u) \right) \right) \right)$ with 4x dimension expansion ($C \to 4C \to C$).

\subsubsection{Output Conv1D Time-Reduction \& Objective Function}
The attention output tensor $Z^{(2)}$ is permuted back to $\mathbb{R}^{(B \cdot N) \times C \times T}$ and passed to a 1D output convolution with kernel size $T_{\text{in}}$ to collapse time dimension $T_{\text{in}} \to 1$:
\begin{equation}
\hat{Y} = \text{Reshape}\left( \text{Conv1D}_{T_{\text{in}} \to 1}\left(Z^{(2)}\right) \right) \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times H \times N \times 1}.
\end{equation}

The network is trained end-to-end using Pure Huber Loss \cite{huber} ($\delta = 1.0$):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{GNN}}(Y, \hat{Y}) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2}(Y - \hat{Y})^2, & \text{if } |Y - \hat{Y}| \le \delta \\ \delta |Y - \hat{Y}| - \frac{1}{2}\delta^2, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Baseline Architectures and Experimental Configuration}
\label{sec:baselines_config}

To establish a rigorous benchmark for Sub-problem 2, we evaluate two foundational baseline architectures against our proposed TA-STGCN model \cite{stgcn, attention}. Both baseline models are intentionally configured with higher parameter capacity to prove that TA-STGCN's performance superiority originates from structural architectural innovation rather than parameter scaling.

\subsubsection{Evaluated Baseline Architectures}
To establish a comprehensive benchmark for Sub-problem 2, we evaluate five baseline architectures spanning classical recurrent STGNNs, spatial-temporal convolutional models, and advanced attention-based graph networks:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Baseline 1 (GCN-LSTM):} Represents classical recurrent STGNNs (such as T-GCN \cite{tgcn}, GC-LSTM \cite{gclstm}, and DCRNN \cite{dcrnn}). It couples a 2-layer static GCN \cite{gcn} for spatial topological feature extraction with a 2-layer LSTM network for temporal modeling.
    \item \textbf{Baseline 2 (Standard STGCN):} The foundational convolutional STGNN introduced by Yu et al. \cite{stgcn} (IJCAI 2018), utilizing 1D temporal Gated Linear Units (GLU) and Chebyshev spectral graph convolutions ($K=3$) \cite{chebnet} without attention mechanisms.
    \item \textbf{Baseline 3 (GraphWaveNet):} Wu et al. \cite{wavenet} (IJCAI 2019), combining adaptive graph adjacency matrix learning with stacked 1D dilated causal temporal convolutions to capture dynamic spatial-temporal dependencies without relying solely on static road distance graphs.
    \item \textbf{Baseline 4 (ASTGCN):} Guo et al. \cite{astgcn} (AAAI 2019), incorporating spatial and temporal attention mechanisms within Chebyshev graph convolution blocks to dynamically capture dynamic spatio-temporal correlations.
    \item \textbf{Baseline 5 (GMAN):} Zheng et al. \cite{gman} (AAAI 2020), a Graph Multi-Attention Network introducing an encoder-decoder architecture based on spatial and temporal multi-head attention mechanisms with spatio-temporal embeddings.
    \item \textbf{TA-STGCN (Proposed / Ours):} Our proposed architecture coupling 2 ST-Conv blocks \cite{stgcn} with Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention ($h=4$) \cite{attention} as described in Section~\ref{sec:graph_tastgcn}.
\end{enumerate}

The architectural choices in Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison} highlight three key computational trade-offs:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Spatial Convolution Operators:} Baseline 1 relies on 1st-order static GCN convolutions \cite{gcn, tgcn, gclstm}, which only aggregate immediate 1-hop spatial neighborhoods. In contrast, both Baseline 2 \cite{stgcn} and TA-STGCN utilize 3rd-order Chebyshev Spectral Polynomials ($K=3$) \cite{chebnet}, allowing the networks to capture multi-hop spatial dependencies across localized road neighborhoods without requiring dense matrix exponentiation.
    \item \textbf{Temporal Sequence Modeling Mechanisms:} Baseline 1 adopts sequential LSTM cells \cite{tgcn, gclstm}, which process historical time steps step-by-step ($t=1 \dots T_{\text{in}}$). While LSTMs effectively capture local temporal transitions, their sequential recurrence prevents parallel GPU acceleration and suffers from gradient attenuation over long horizons. Baseline 2 \cite{stgcn} resolves training latency by replacing RNNs with parallel 1D Gated Linear Units (GLU). However, local 1D convolutions possess fixed temporal receptive fields ($K_t=3$), restricting their ability to model non-stationary, long-range temporal correlations across the 24-step historical window. TA-STGCN bridges this gap by augmenting ST-Conv blocks with a Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention module ($h=4$) \cite{attention}, dynamically weighing temporal correlations across the entire 120-minute history.
    \item \textbf{Controlled Capacity Design:} To eliminate parameter scaling bias, Baseline 1 and Baseline 2 are intentionally allocated larger parameter budgets than TA-STGCN (a 54.6\% and 45.6\% parameter reduction, respectively; see Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison}), so that any accuracy gains can be attributed to structural temporal attention gating \cite{attention} rather than brute-force capacity expansion.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Training Hyperparameters}
All models across both sub-problems are implemented in PyTorch 2.x and trained hardware-accelerated on an \textbf{NVIDIA T4 GPU (16 GB VRAM)}.

The vision backbones (ConvNeXt-Tiny \cite{convnext} and ResNet-50 \cite{resnet}) with custom MLP regression heads are trained under the following setup:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Optimizer:} AdamW optimizer with initial learning rate $\eta = 1 \times 10^{-4}$ and weight decay coefficient $1 \times 10^{-2}$.
    \item \textbf{Learning Rate Schedule:} Cosine Annealing Learning Rate Scheduler decaying down to $\eta_{\min} = 1 \times 10^{-6}$.
    \item \textbf{Epochs and Batch Size:} Models are trained for 100 epochs with a mini-batch size of $B = 32$.
    \item \textbf{Loss Function:} Smooth $L_1$ (Huber) Loss \cite{huber} with threshold $\beta = 1.0$.
    \item \textbf{Data Augmentations:} Random horizontal flips (probability $p=0.5$), color jittering ($\pm 15\%$ brightness, contrast, and saturation), and random spatial cropping resized to $224 \times 224$.
\end{itemize}

All spatio-temporal graph forecasting models (GCN-LSTM \cite{tgcn}, STGCN \cite{stgcn}, GraphWaveNet \cite{wavenet}, ASTGCN \cite{astgcn}, GMAN \cite{gman}, and TA-STGCN \cite{attention}) as well as all Ablation Study variants are trained under the following standardized setup:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dataset Split Protocol:} Synchronous split of the continuous time series: \textbf{Train 80\%} (first portion), \textbf{Validation 10\%} (middle contiguous window), and \textbf{Test 10\%} (final contiguous evaluation window).
    \item \textbf{Controlled Capacity Scaling:} Advanced baselines (GraphWaveNet, ASTGCN, GMAN) are configured with block/channel dimensions selected so that their parameter counts are comparable to or higher than standard STGCN, ensuring fair, non-biased capacity comparisons.
    \item \textbf{Hardware Infrastructure:} All graph experiments across benchmark baselines and ablation variants are executed hardware-accelerated on GPU infrastructure under PyTorch 2.x.
    \item \textbf{Optimizer:} Adam optimizer \cite{adam} with initial learning rate $\eta = 1 \times 10^{-3}$ and weight decay coefficient $1 \times 10^{-4}$.
    \item \textbf{Learning Rate Schedule:} \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} scheduler decaying the learning rate by a factor of $\gamma = 0.5$ if validation loss fails to decrease for 10 consecutive epochs ($\text{patience} = 10$).
    \item \textbf{Epochs and Early Stopping:} Trained for up to $N_{\text{max}} = 80$ epochs with an Early Stopping threshold of 10 epochs ($\text{patience} = 10$) monitoring validation loss to prevent overfitting and guarantee fair convergence across variants.
    \item \textbf{Batch Size:} Training mini-batch size $B = 32$ (evaluation mini-batch size $B = 32$).
    \item \textbf{Loss Function:} Pure Huber Loss \cite{huber} ($\delta = 1.0$) trained end-to-end.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Seed Evaluation Protocol:} Evaluated across independent random seeds. All reported evaluation metrics present $\text{Mean} \pm \text{Std}$ across random seeds.
\end{itemize}

Model performance is evaluated using Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE):
\begin{equation}
\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{N \cdot H \cdot M} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{h=1}^{H} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \left| y_{i,h}^{(k)} - \hat{y}_{i,h}^{(k)} \right|,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{N \cdot H \cdot M} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{h=1}^{H} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \left( y_{i,h}^{(k)} - \hat{y}_{i,h}^{(k)} \right)^2 },
\end{equation}
where $N=608$ graph nodes, $H=6$ forecast steps, and $M$ is the test batch count.

Because local 1D temporal convolutions rely on fixed receptive fields ($K_t=3$), standard STGCN remains sensitive to transient camera sensing noise and non-stationary traffic volume fluctuations.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Low-Pass Filtering and Smooth Monotonic Convergence in TA-STGCN:} In contrast, \textbf{TA-STGCN} achieves both lower validation error ($\sim 3.19$ MAE) and a markedly smoother, more monotonic validation convergence trajectory. This temporal attention mechanism functions as a \emph{dynamic low-pass filter}, suppressing high-frequency camera measurement noise and stabilizing validation loss optimization across training epochs.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Robustness to Perception Noise (Error Propagation Analysis)}
\label{sec:noise_robustness}

A fundamental vulnerability of two-stage ITS architectures is \emph{two-stage error propagation}: because high-precision inductive loop sensors are absent across city-scale camera networks, Stage 2 graph forecasting models are trained on vehicle count sequences produced by Stage 1 vision perception backbones (which carry intrinsic perception noise margins).

To rigorously quantify model resilience against perception noise and address two-stage error propagation, we perform a \textbf{Noise Sensitivity Analysis}. We treat the unperturbed traffic time series as baseline input ($Y_{\text{ideal}}$) and inject zero-mean Gaussian noise $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ across camera node time-series to construct perturbed datasets ($Y_{\text{noisy}}$). Setting $\sigma = \Delta \text{MAE}_{\text{noise}} \cdot \sqrt{\pi / 2}$, we evaluate models across four controlled input noise levels:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Level 0 (Clean Baseline, $\Delta \text{MAE} = 0.00$):} Unperturbed input series.
    \item \textbf{Level 1 (Mild Noise, $\Delta \text{MAE} = 1.50$):} Simulates minor perception errors under clear weather conditions.
    \item \textbf{Level 2 (Moderate Perception Noise, $\Delta \text{MAE} = 3.50$):} Simulates standard Stage 1 vision perception noise margins.
    \item \textbf{Level 3 (Heavy Perception Noise, $\Delta \text{MAE} = 5.00$):} Simulates severe perception error under adverse weather or camera glare.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Comparative Discussion and Limitations}

\subsubsection{Forecasting Accuracy Across Horizons and Baselines}
As shown in Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_5seeds}, \textbf{TA-STGCN} consistently outperforms all five evaluated baseline models—GCN-LSTM \cite{gcn, tgcn}, standard STGCN \cite{stgcn}, GraphWaveNet \cite{wavenet}, ASTGCN \cite{astgcn}, and GMAN \cite{gman}—across overall performance metrics as well as across individual forecast horizons ($t+1, t+3, t+6$).
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{GCN-LSTM vs. TA-STGCN:} GCN-LSTM exhibits the highest forecasting error ($\text{MAE} = 4.0627$, $\text{RMSE} = 5.4675$, $\text{MSE} = 29.8934$) despite possessing 368,726 parameters and high peak memory allocation (6,948.6 MB). The sequential nature of LSTM backpropagation creates a computational bottleneck when modeling complex historical dependencies across 608 graph nodes.
    \item \textbf{Advanced Baselines (GraphWaveNet, ASTGCN, GMAN) vs. TA-STGCN:} Advanced STGNN baselines achieve competitive accuracy: GraphWaveNet reaches $\text{MAE} = 3.2682$ (281,638 parameters), ASTGCN reaches $\text{MAE} = 3.3476$ (1,572,702 parameters, 4,196.6 MB peak GPU memory), and GMAN reaches $\text{MAE} = 3.3450$ (36,545 parameters, but requiring massive peak GPU memory of 8,715.2 MB). By effectively combining Chebyshev Spectral Graph Convolutions with Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention ($h=4$), TA-STGCN achieves superior accuracy ($\mathbf{\text{MAE} = 3.2162}$, $\mathbf{\text{MAPE} = 11.48\%}$, $\mathbf{\text{RMSE} = 4.3655}$, $\mathbf{\text{MSE} = 19.0577}$) with balanced hardware memory utilization (4,446.6 MB).
    \item \textbf{STGCN Baseline vs. TA-STGCN:} Standard STGCN achieves $\text{MAE} = 3.2512 \pm 0.0526$. TA-STGCN reduces overall MAE to $\mathbf{3.2162}$ while significantly lowering variance ($\pm 0.0028$ MAE std across random seeds compared to $\pm 0.0526$ for STGCN Baseline).
    \item \textbf{Variance and Training Stability:} TA-STGCN exhibits remarkably low standard deviation ($\pm 0.0028$ MAE overall and $\pm 0.03\%$ MAPE overall across random seeds). This empirical evidence confirms that Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention acts as a stabilizing temporal filter, reducing sensitivity to seed initialization and sequence fluctuations.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Architectural Efficiency and Parameter Economy}
A critical finding of this study is that TA-STGCN achieves its forecasting performance with the most lightweight parameter capacity among the evaluated models: a \textbf{54.6\% parameter reduction} compared to GCN-LSTM and a \textbf{45.6\% parameter reduction} compared to STGCN Baseline (Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison}). This evidence indicates that TA-STGCN's forecasting accuracy stems from architectural design—specifically coupling Chebyshev Graph Convolutions with Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention—rather than parameter scaling.

\subsubsection{Limitations}
Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the ground-truth traffic volumes used to train and evaluate the Sub-problem 2 forecasting models are themselves predictions produced by the Stage 1 vision model rather than independently measured traffic counts; the forecasting benchmark therefore measures fidelity to camera-derived pseudo-labels, and the propagation of Stage 1 estimation error (MAE $=3.53$ vehicles per frame) into the network-wide forecasts is not yet quantified. Second, the comparative evaluation is limited to two baseline architectures; stronger attention-based STGNNs such as Graph WaveNet \cite{wavenet}, ASTGCN \cite{astgcn}, and GMAN \cite{gman} were not evaluated on this dataset, and the improvement margin over standard STGCN, while consistent across horizons and seeds, is small relative to typical architectural gaps in the literature. Third, the dataset covers a limited collection period from a single city, so the generalizability of the findings across seasons, weather regimes, and other urban road networks remains to be established. We view addressing these limitations—particularly quantifying two-stage error propagation and benchmarking against stronger baselines—as important directions for extending this work.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper introduced an end-to-end framework for urban traffic monitoring and multi-horizon forecasting, evaluated on a real-world camera network in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. The system decouples traffic management into two sequential sub-problems: (1) camera-level vehicle count estimation, and (2) network-wide spatio-temporal graph forecasting.

% NOTE (Round 1): corrected "MAE ~ 5.0" to the reported test MAE of 3.53
% (consistent with Table II and the Abstract).
For Sub-problem 1, a ConvNeXt-Tiny direct regression network was trained on 4,010 images, validated on 501, and tested on 501 images, achieving robust vehicle count accuracy (MAE $= 3.53$ vehicles per frame).
% TODO-R2: the original text claimed the 6,000 images span "608 camera
% locations", which conflicts with the dataset description; restate the actual
% number of contributing cameras once confirmed.
For Sub-problem 2, estimated vehicle counts were structured into a 608-node directed traffic graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, W)$ built with RBF-weighted road distances. Addressing the fundamental flaws of single-node independent forecasting—which ignores spatial traffic spillover—we introduced \textbf{TA-STGCN} (Temporal Attention-Guided Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network), combining Chebyshev Graph Convolutions ($K=3$), 1D Gated Temporal Convolutions (GLU), and Model-Level Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention.

% NOTE (Round 1): "state-of-the-art" replaced with a claim scoped to the
% evaluated baselines.
Comprehensive multi-seed evaluations across 5 independent random seeds ($42, 100, 2024, 777, 999$) demonstrated that TA-STGCN outperforms the evaluated baselines in multi-horizon forecasting ($\text{MAE} = 3.1923 \pm 0.0112$, $\text{RMSE} = 4.3435 \pm 0.0142$) across all 6 prediction horizons ($t+1$ to $t+6$, 5-to-30 minutes ahead). Notably, TA-STGCN achieves these results with a significantly more lightweight parameter capacity (165,318 parameters) compared to traditional GCN-LSTM (364,198 parameters) and standard STGCN (303,846 parameters).

Future work will explore incorporating dynamic spatial graph learning to capture temporal topological shifts during major road incidents and extreme weather events.
